When the model is a material service provider: AI resilience for an Australian insurer under CPS 230

An Australian general insurer had put AI into claims intake, customer service and fraud review, mostly through one hosted model provider. Nobody had asked what happens when that provider is slow, out of capacity or gone. Under APRA's CPS 230 that is a board-level question, and the goal was a credible answer.

The situation

A general insurer in Australia had moved quickly with AI. Claims intake used a hosted model to read documents and photos, the contact centre used it to draft responses, and the fraud team used it to summarise case files. Almost all of it ran through one external model provider, wired directly into each application.

CPS 230, APRA's prudential standard on operational risk management, has applied to banks, insurers and superannuation entities since 1 July 2025, with existing service provider contracts brought in at renewal or by 1 July 20261. It expects entities to identify critical operations, set tolerance levels for disruption, and manage material service providers2. The board asked a fair question: if the model provider has an outage, runs out of capacity or changes terms, what happens to claims?

Why now. Capacity is not a theoretical risk. Reporting on Microsoft's latest results notes that demand for its cloud still exceeds supply4, and AI capacity is the tightest part of that market. See the tech news update for 29 October.

What had to be true

  • AI dependencies visible in the same register as other material service providers.
  • Tolerance levels for each AI-assisted critical operation: how long, how degraded, before customers are harmed.
  • A tested alternative for every critical use, not a slide.
  • Privacy obligations met on every path, including fallbacks.
  • No rebuild of the applications: changes behind a common interface.

The hard part: deciding what to drop when capacity is short

Not every AI use is equally important, and pretending otherwise means everything fails together. The team tagged each request with the criticality of the operation it served, so the gateway could protect claims intake and shed or delay lower-priority work. The same idea appears in the Kubernetes Gateway API inference extension, where scheduling considers whether a request is critical and whether there is capacity before choosing a model server, and can drop non-critical requests when there is not5.

Resilience for AI is mostly a prioritisation decision. Decide in advance which work gets the last available capacity.

Every fallback path also had to meet privacy obligations. The OAIC's guidance on commercially available AI products expects due diligence on each product and ongoing review, not a one-off check3, so the second provider and the private model went through the same assessment as the primary. Routing patterns are covered in LLM model routing and Kubernetes inference gateways.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: put a gateway in front of every model call before adding a second provider; it made everything else cheap. Differently: build the evaluation set for each critical operation first. The slowest part of adding a provider was proving its answers were good enough, not wiring it in. See evaluation sets that predict production and AI incident postmortems.

FAQ

Common questions

Does CPS 230 apply to AI model providers?

CPS 230 covers operational risk, business continuity and the management of service providers for APRA-regulated entities. If an AI provider supports a critical operation, it belongs in that framework like any other material service provider; how your arrangements are classified is a question for your risk and legal teams.

What is a degraded mode for an AI-assisted process?

A defined way to keep the operation running when the model is unavailable: a second provider, a smaller private model, or rules and human handling, with known limits on volume and quality.

How do you test an exit plan for a model provider?

Run real traffic for an operation through the alternative path for a set period, measure quality, cost and latency against tolerance levels, and record what broke. A plan that has never carried traffic is not a plan.
