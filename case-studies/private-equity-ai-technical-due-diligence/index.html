Testing whether an AI company's technology is what the deck says it is

An investor was close to committing to a company whose value rested on its AI. The questions were simple to state and hard to answer: is the AI really theirs, do they have the rights to the data behind it, will it scale at a sensible cost, and can this team keep building it?

The situation

A private equity investor was preparing to invest in a software company whose growth story rested on AI: models that automated part of its customers' work and data assets that, the deck said, competitors could not replicate. The commercial and financial diligence was well under way. What the deal team lacked was an independent view of whether the technology, the data rights and the team matched the story, and what it would cost to fix anything that did not.

The timeline was set by the deal, so the review had to be structured, evidence-led and focused on findings that could change price, terms or the post-close plan.

What had to be true

  • Evidence over assertion: every finding tied to something inspected, not only something said in an interview.
  • Proprietary versus borrowed made explicit: which capabilities the company built, and which depend on third-party models or APIs.
  • Data rights traced from source to model to product, including customer data used for training.
  • Costs that scale: inference and cloud spend modelled against the growth plan.
  • A report the deal team can use: risk-rated, with recommended responses, readable by non-engineers.

Architecture

The review was organised into workstreams, each with its own evidence requests and interview plan. AI governance was assessed against the functions of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (govern, map, measure, manage)1, with ISO/IEC 42001 used as the reference for what a management system for AI looks like2. The comparison is in ISO 42001 vs NIST AI RMF.

The data flow, end to end

Secure development practices were reviewed against the NIST Secure Software Development Framework, which groups practices into preparing the organisation, protecting the software, producing well-secured software and responding to vulnerabilities3. The full checklist I use is in the AI startup technical due diligence checklist, and supply chain questions in AI model supply chain security.

The hard part: proving data rights, not just data

Showing that a company has data is easy. Showing that it may use that data for the purpose the plan depends on is not. The provenance workstream asked, for each dataset behind a model:

  • Where it came from, under which contract or licence, and whether that permits training and commercial use.
  • Whether customer data was used to train shared models, and whether customer contracts allow it.
  • Whether the dataset is documented in a form like a datasheet, covering motivation, composition and collection4.
  • Whether models carry documentation of intended use and evaluation, in the spirit of model cards5.

Regulation was mapped to where the company sells. For customers in the European Union, the review considered how the EU AI Act might classify the product and which provider or deployer obligations could apply6.

In AI diligence, the most valuable finding is often not a technical flaw but a missing right. A gap in data permissions can matter more to value than any code quality issue.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: insist on repository and billing access early, and separate verified findings from representations in the report. Differently: start the data rights tracing on the first day, in parallel with legal counsel. It is the slowest workstream and the one most likely to change the deal.

FAQ

Common questions

What does AI technical due diligence cover that ordinary tech DD does not?

It adds model and data provenance, rights to training and customer data, dependence on third-party models, evaluation evidence, inference cost behaviour and AI-specific governance and regulation.

Do you need access to the code?

Read access to repositories, or a supervised session, gives far stronger evidence than documents and interviews alone. Where access is limited, the report says which findings rest on representations rather than inspection.

What does a risk-rated report contain?

Each finding has a severity, the evidence behind it, its likely impact on value or the plan, and a recommended response such as a price adjustment, a condition, a warranty or a post-close action.
