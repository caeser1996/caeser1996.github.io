Joining quality, production and supplier data so defects surface earlier

Quality data in one system, production data in each plant's own stack, supplier data in spreadsheets and portals. Defects were found late because nobody could join the three. The goal was an open lakehouse fed by contracted data from every plant, with quality metrics everyone agreed on.

The situation

A manufacturer with several plants had good systems and poor connections. Quality inspection results lived in a central quality system. Production parameters lived in each plant's execution and historian stack, configured differently at every site. Supplier certificates and incoming inspection data arrived as spreadsheets and portal exports. When a customer complaint came in, tracing it back to a material lot, a line, a shift and a process setting took days of manual joining, and by then more product had shipped.

Previous attempts at a central warehouse had stalled. Every plant change broke a pipeline, and every report defined "first-pass yield" slightly differently.

What had to be true

  • Joinable at the right grain: batch, lot, line and time, across quality, production and supplier data.
  • Plants accountable for their feeds, with a clear owner and a written promise for each.
  • Open storage, so different teams and engines could use the same tables.
  • One definition per metric, shared by dashboards, analysts and any future AI use.
  • No rip and replace of plant systems.

ISA-95 framed the boundaries. It addresses the interface between enterprise business systems and manufacturing operations and control1, which is exactly where the lakehouse sat: reading from plant-level systems, serving enterprise-level analysis, owning neither.

Architecture

Apache Iceberg tracks table state through metadata files replaced by atomic swaps, with snapshots and schema evolution defined in the specification2. That gave reproducible reads for investigations and safe schema changes when a plant added a parameter. See Apache Iceberg v3 for the lakehouse and lakehouse catalog strategy.

The hard part: getting plants to own a promise

The technology was well understood. The hard part was organisational: plants had never been asked to promise anything about their data, and a central team had always absorbed the breakage. Contracts changed that, but only once they were written in terms plants recognised.

We used the Open Data Contract Standard, a YAML-based, platform-neutral format for agreements between data producers and consumers3; version 3 organises a contract into sections that include schema, data quality, service levels and team4. In practice:

  • Contracts started small: schema, owner, freshness and a handful of rules the plant itself agreed were reasonable.
  • A failed check went to the plant's named owner, not to the central team's backlog.
  • Breaking changes required a new contract version and a notice period, agreed in a short change process.
  • Plant-specific codes stayed in the feed; mapping to shared reference data was a central, versioned step.

A data contract is an organisational tool that happens to be written in YAML. Its value is the conversation it forces between producer and consumer.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: start by tracing one real complaint end to end, and make plants owners rather than suppliers of data. Differently: agree metric definitions with quality leads before building dashboards, not alongside them. Every disagreement found late cost a rebuild.

FAQ

Common questions

Why an open table format for manufacturing data?

Plants and teams use different engines and tools. An open table format such as Apache Iceberg lets them read and write the same tables with consistent snapshots and schema evolution, without locking the data into one vendor's engine.

What does a data contract add?

A contract states what a producer promises: schema, semantics, freshness and quality checks, plus an owner. It turns silent breaking changes into failed checks at the boundary, where the producing team can fix them.

Do plants need to replace their MES or quality systems?

No. The lakehouse reads from existing systems through change data capture or extracts. The contract sits on the interface, not inside each plant's applications.
