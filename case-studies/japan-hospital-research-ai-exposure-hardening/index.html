One front door for research: securing lab-deployed AI tools at a Japanese university hospital

A Japanese university hospital ran research AI tools that individual labs had installed themselves: imaging annotation servers, notebook servers, model demos. Some were reachable from the internet, outside the hospital's security operations. When AI agents were reported to have found and exploited a zero-day in a medical imaging tool on exactly that kind of server, the goal became clear: find every research service, put it behind one front door, and do it without stopping the research.

The situation

A Japanese university hospital had a strong central IT function for clinical systems and a very different world in its research labs. Radiology, pathology and informatics groups ran their own AI tools: imaging annotation servers for labelling studies, notebook servers for model development, demo applications for collaborators. Some sat on lab machines, some on cloud accounts paid from research grants, and some were reachable from the internet so partners at other institutions could log in.

The hospital followed the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's guidelines on the safety management of medical information systems, whose version 6.0 was restructured to respond to cyberattacks and wider cloud use, with separate volumes for governance, management and system operations3. Those controls were well applied to clinical systems. Research services were largely outside them.

Why now. In November 2026, Amazon reported that a suspected China-nexus operator used autonomous AI agents to map the API of MONAI Label, an open-source medical imaging annotation tool, find a path traversal flaw and exploit it on university servers in East Asia1. The Register reported the agents went on to gain root2. The hospital's research groups used tools of exactly this kind. The board asked how many such servers were exposed. Nobody could answer.

What had to be true

  • Every research service known, with an owner, purpose and the data it can reach.
  • Nothing research-related directly reachable from the internet; one authenticated front door instead.
  • External collaborators still able to work, with their own identities, not shared passwords.
  • Patient data separated from experimental tools, with only de-identified data in research zones by default.
  • Research not stopped: labs keep the freedom to deploy, inside guard rails.

Architecture

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: central control without central bottlenecks

The easy answer, making central IT deploy every research tool, would have stopped research and pushed labs back to their own servers. The design had to separate who controls the front door from who controls each service. Kubernetes Gateway API is built around exactly that split: infrastructure providers define gateway classes, cluster operators run gateways, and application developers attach their own routes5.

In practice, the hospital's platform team ran the gateway and its authentication; each lab had a namespace and could publish routes to its own tools, which the gateway only accepted with an access policy attached. A new annotation server took the same steps for a lab as before, but could no longer appear on the internet without login.

The fastest win came before any platform work: an external scan of the hospital's address ranges and research cloud accounts. It turned an unknown into a list with owners.

Data was the second half. The ministry also publishes cyber security measures for medical institutions alongside the guidelines4, and the hospital extended the same thinking to research. Research tools received de-identified exports by default; identifiable data stayed in a separate zone that required approval and was never reachable from tools exposed to external collaborators. The pattern follows the health evidence platform and federated health analytics, and the gateway approach builds on moving to Gateway API.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: start with an external scan and a service register; it changed the conversation from fear to a list. Differently: bring lab leads in as co-designers of the platform from the first week. Researchers adopted it fastest where they had shaped the deployment templates. See AI-assisted intrusions and the tech news for 22 November.

FAQ

Common questions

What happened with MONAI Label?

Amazon reported that a suspected China-nexus operator's autonomous AI agents mapped the API of MONAI Label, an open-source medical imaging annotation tool, found a path traversal flaw and exploited it against university servers in East Asia.

Why are research tools a particular risk in hospitals?

They are often installed by individual labs, outside central IT, on servers that are reachable for collaborators, and they can sit near imaging or clinical data. They are rarely patched or monitored like clinical systems.

Does this mean banning lab-deployed tools?

No. The aim is a platform where labs can still deploy what they need, but only behind central authentication, on a segmented network, with monitoring and an owner for every service.
