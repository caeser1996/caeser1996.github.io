Ready for 10 December: automated decision transparency for an Australian energy retailer

An energy retailer used software to run credit checks, set payment plans, flag hardship and trigger collections, across systems built over twenty years. From 10 December 2026, Australian organisations must explain in their privacy policy when computer programs make or substantially inform decisions that significantly affect people. The retailer first had to find out where that happened.

The situation

An Australian energy retailer served households and small businesses across several states. Software touched many decisions that matter to customers: credit checks at sign-up, security deposits, payment plan offers, hardship flags, collections steps and, at the end of the line, disconnection warnings. The logic lived in a billing platform from the 2000s, a CRM, a collections tool, a credit bureau integration and a newer machine learning model that predicted who might struggle to pay.

Why now. From 10 December 2026, APP entities must include information in their privacy policy when they have arranged for a computer program to make, or do something substantially and directly related to making, decisions that significantly affect individuals' rights or interests1. The OAIC published its final guidance on 30 September 20264. Commentary on that guidance reads it broadly: it reaches rules engines and decision-support tools, not only AI, and human review does not automatically take a decision out of scope23. The retailer's privacy policy said nothing about any of this, and nobody could list where it applied.

What had to be true

  • A complete list of significant decisions that software makes or substantially informs.
  • Disclosures that match reality, generated from that list and kept current.
  • Evidence for each decision: which system, which logic version, which data.
  • Explanations on request that customer service can give in plain language.
  • No new hidden decisions: changes to decision logic registered before release.

The hard part: finding decisions in twenty-year-old systems

Nobody had a list. Decision logic sat in billing configuration tables, stored procedures, collections scripts and a model in a notebook pipeline. Interviews found the obvious decisions; data found the rest. The team traced customer journeys that ended in a significant outcome, such as a deposit request or a disconnection warning, back through the systems that produced them, and recorded every step where software chose or ranked something.

Capturing evidence without rewriting the billing platform was the second problem. The OpenTelemetry Collector can read existing log files and query databases through receivers, then forward everything in one format, which is how the OpenTelemetry community recommends bringing older systems into modern observability5. The team used it to turn existing logs and table changes into structured decision events, with no change to the legacy code.

The scope calls were made with counsel. Following the guidance's broad reading, the team treated borderline cases as in scope, including the payment difficulty model, even though a person made the final hardship decision23. The hardship model was also a reason for care: it informed help for vulnerable customers, so its explanations were written with the hardship team. See explainable credit decisions in Brazil for the reason-code pattern.

You cannot disclose what you have not found. The privacy policy paragraph is the last step; the inventory is the work.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: trace from outcomes backwards. It found decisions no interview would have surfaced, such as a deposit rule hidden in a billing configuration table. Differently: start the release gate first. Two new rules went live during the inventory and had to be found again. FAQ

Common questions

What changes on 10 December 2026?

APP entities must include information in their privacy policy where they have arranged for a computer program to make, or do something substantially and directly related to making, decisions that significantly affect individuals, using personal information. The duty comes from the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024.

Does human review take a decision out of scope?

Not automatically. According to commentary on the OAIC's guidance, a decision can be in scope even when a program's output only informs a person who makes the final call, and the OAIC encourages treating borderline cases as in scope.

Is this only about AI?

No. The OAIC's guidance reaches rules engines, scoring tools and other software that substantially informs significant decisions, not only machine learning.
