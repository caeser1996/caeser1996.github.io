Change without fear: progressive rollouts and fast rollback for a Brazilian payments platform

A Brazilian payment institution processed more than a million transactions a day, most of them instant payments that customers expect to complete in seconds. Most of its incidents were not caused by attackers or hardware, but by its own changes: a configuration tweak, a library upgrade, a network adjustment. After a major provider's long outage was traced to a routine network change exposing a latent bug, the company decided to make every change small, observable and reversible.

The situation

A Brazilian payment institution offered accounts, cards and instant payments to consumers and small businesses, processing more than a million transactions a day. Customers expected instant payments to complete in seconds at any hour, and partners expected the same. The platform ran on Kubernetes across two cloud regions, with dozens of services owned by several teams.

Its post-incident reviews told a consistent story. The serious incidents rarely came from attacks or hardware. They came from the company's own changes: a configuration value changed everywhere at once, a library upgrade that behaved differently under peak load, a network rule adjusted during maintenance. Code deployments had some safeguards; configuration and network changes had almost none.

Brazil's National Monetary Council requires regulated institutions to maintain a cybersecurity policy with incident response and to manage risks in data processing and cloud services5. The company's resilience depended as much on how it changed its own systems as on any external threat.

Why now. In November 2026, Microsoft attributed a long Microsoft 365 outage to traffic shifts during routine network maintenance exposing a latent bug from an earlier capacity upgrade12. If one of the world's largest operators could be caught by a routine network change, the board asked, what protected a payments platform from the same pattern?

What had to be true

  • Every change counted: code, configuration, infrastructure and network changes all go through a controlled path.
  • Small first: changes reach a small slice of traffic before everyone.
  • Judged by customers: health measured by payment success and latency, not server metrics alone.
  • Reversible: rollback is fast, rehearsed and the default when in doubt.
  • Visible: anyone can see what changed, where and when, during an incident.
  • Still fast: teams ship often; safety comes from smaller changes, not fewer.

Architecture

Code deployments moved to progressive rollouts on Kubernetes: a new version first replaced a small number of pods while the rest kept serving, and only continued if it stayed healthy. Google's SRE guidance describes canarying in exactly these terms: test each change on a small slice of traffic so defects are caught early with limited impact3.

The bigger change was bringing configuration and network changes into the same discipline6. Configuration moved into version control and rolled out by region and by service in stages, with the same health checks as code. Network changes, such as firewall rules, load balancer settings and routing, went through a pipeline that applied them to one zone first and watched payment signals before continuing.

The hard part: judging health in a payments system

Choosing signals that reflect customers. Server metrics often looked fine while customers suffered. Canary analysis used signals that matched customer experience: payment success rate by payment type and partner, end-to-end latency, error codes returned to apps and depth of downstream queues. The SRE workbook stresses choosing metrics that genuinely indicate problems and comparing the canary against a control rather than a fixed threshold3.

Low-volume paths and latent bugs. A canary on a small slice of traffic can miss problems in rare paths, such as an unusual partner or a refund flow, and latent bugs may only appear under peak load. The team added synthetic transactions for rare paths, kept each wave long enough to cover a busy period for critical changes, and avoided large changes during known peaks such as month-end and holidays.

Rollback as the default. Engineers used to try to fix forward during incidents. The new rule: if a recent change might be involved, roll it back first and investigate afterwards. Rollback was rehearsed regularly, and the change timeline appeared on every dashboard so responders could see what had just changed. Google's release engineering guidance treats reliable, repeatable releases and rollbacks as foundations of reliability4.

Most outages are self-inflicted. Make every change small, watched and reversible, including the configuration and network changes nobody thinks of as releases.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: put every change, of every type, on one timeline overlaid on dashboards; it shortened incidents before any automation existed. Differently: bring network engineering in from the start rather than after the code pipeline was done; network changes turned out to carry the most risk and the least tooling. FAQ

Common questions

What is a canary release?

Rolling a change out to a small share of traffic or servers first, comparing its behaviour against the existing version on key signals, and only continuing if it is healthy. Google's SRE guidance describes canarying as a way to catch defects early while limiting impact.

Why include configuration and network changes in progressive rollout?

Because many serious outages come from configuration and network changes rather than code. Microsoft's November 2026 Microsoft 365 outage, for example, was attributed to network maintenance exposing a latent bug from an earlier upgrade.

What signals should decide whether a payments canary is healthy?

Customer-facing signals: payment success rate, end-to-end latency, error rates by payment type and partner, and downstream queue depth, rather than only server CPU or memory.
