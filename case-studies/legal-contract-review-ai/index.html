Helping lawyers review contracts against the playbook without giving up the final word

Every incoming contract was compared to the playbook by hand, clause by clause, under deadline. The goal was not to replace that judgement but to prepare it: extract the clauses, compare them to the playbook, and propose cited redlines a lawyer accepts, edits or rejects.

The situation

A legal team handled a steady flow of commercial agreements: supplier contracts, NDAs, services agreements, licences. Each one was checked against an internal playbook that set out preferred positions, acceptable fallbacks and red lines for clauses such as liability caps, indemnities, termination, governing law and data protection. The work was skilled but repetitive, and it bunched up at quarter end when everything was urgent.

The team had tried generic AI chat tools. The answers sounded plausible, cited nothing, and didn't know the playbook. Nobody trusted them, rightly.

What had to be true

  • The lawyer decides. The system prepares and proposes; it never sends a redline on its own.
  • Every suggestion is cited to the exact contract text and the playbook position it relies on.
  • Consistent structure, so outputs can be validated and compared, not just read.
  • Measured accuracy per clause type, on the team's own contracts, before anyone relies on it.
  • Confidentiality: contract text handled under the firm's existing data controls.

Contract review is a well-studied task: the CUAD dataset, built with legal experts, frames it as finding the clauses a reviewer needs to see across dozens of categories1. That framing, extraction first and judgement second, shaped the design.

Architecture

Extraction used structured outputs, where the model's response is constrained to a supplied JSON Schema2. Each clause came back with its type, section reference, verbatim quote and confidence. A deterministic check then confirmed that every quote actually appeared in the source document. See structured outputs for reliable LLM integration.

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: long context versus retrieval

A single contract usually fits within a modern model's context window, so the temptation is to put everything in: the contract, the whole playbook, and a pile of precedents. Research on long contexts shows models use information at the start and end of the input more reliably than information in the middle3, which is a poor property when the clause that matters is on page fourteen.

The design split the jobs:

  • The contract is read whole for extraction, because cross-references and definitions matter.
  • The playbook is retrieved per clause, in the spirit of retrieval-augmented generation4, so each comparison sees only the relevant positions.
  • Comparison runs clause by clause, with small, focused prompts that are easier to evaluate.

The trade-offs are covered in long context vs RAG.

Contracts from counterparties are also untrusted input. Prompt injection tops the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications5, so contract text was always treated as data, the model had no tools that could act on it, and outputs were constrained by schema. See prompt injection defence.

A suggestion a lawyer can verify in ten seconds is worth more than a fluent paragraph they have to check from scratch.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: turn the playbook into data before writing a prompt, and treat lawyer decisions as the evaluation signal. Differently: involve lawyers in schema design from the start. The clause taxonomy is a legal decision, and the earlier they own it, the fewer rework cycles follow.

FAQ

Common questions

Can AI review contracts on its own?

It shouldn't. The reliable pattern is AI preparing the review: extracting clauses, comparing them to the playbook and proposing cited changes, with a lawyer making every decision.

Why structured outputs for clause extraction?

A schema forces the model to return the same fields every time, such as clause type, location, quoted text and playbook position, so the output can be validated, compared and displayed without fragile parsing.

Long context or RAG for contracts?

Both, for different jobs. The contract under review usually fits in context and should be read whole; the playbook, fallback positions and precedents are better retrieved selectively.
