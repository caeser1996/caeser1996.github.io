Results, records and research: a consent-aware data platform for an Indian diagnostics chain

A diagnostics chain ran hundreds of collection centres and several regional labs. Patients wanted results in their digital health records, doctors wanted trends, and the business wanted to use its data for research and AI. India's data protection rules arrive in phases through 2027. The platform had to make every use of a patient's data traceable to a purpose and, where needed, to consent.

The situation

A diagnostics chain in India ran collection centres, home sample collection and several regional laboratories. Results went out as PDFs by email and WhatsApp. Each lab used its own test codes, so a haemoglobin result from one city did not match the same test from another. The analytics team copied production tables into a warehouse with names and phone numbers intact, and the business wanted to start using the data for research partnerships and AI models.

Why now. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified in November 2025 and apply in phases1. The consent manager framework applies from 13 November 2026, and most obligations on data fiduciaries, including notices, consent, data principal rights, breach reporting and children's data, from 13 May 202723. At the same time, the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) gives patients a digital health account and a consent-based way to share records between providers4. The chain wanted to join that ecosystem properly and be ready for the DPDP deadlines in one programme.

What had to be true

  • Purpose for every use: reporting results, billing, analytics, research and AI, each with a recorded basis.
  • Patient-approved sharing of results into digital health records through ABDM.
  • One meaning per test: local codes mapped to a standard terminology once.
  • Research kept apart: pseudonymised, purpose-bound and never mixed with operations.
  • Breach-ready: detection, assessment and reporting routes tested before May 2027.

Architecture

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: one test, many meanings, many purposes

The first problem was meaning. Each lab had grown its own test catalogue, so the same test had different codes and units across cities. Mapping every local code to LOINC once, with the mapping owned by a clinical team and versioned, made results comparable for doctors and usable for research. See clinical terminologies and OMOP vocabularies.

The second was purpose. Operational use, such as reporting a result, rests on a different basis from research. The team kept them physically apart. The research store follows the OMOP Common Data Model, built through a planned extract, transform and load process that starts with the person and visit structure before clinical tables5, and it only ever receives pseudonymised data for recorded purposes. See FHIR to OMOP architecture.

The third was withdrawal. A patient who withdraws consent for research must be removed from future extracts, and the system must be able to show that. Because every research load is driven by the consent service, a withdrawal stops the next load and is recorded against the patient's consent history.

Consent is not a checkbox at registration. It is a record that every pipeline reads before it moves data.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: put clinicians in charge of terminology mapping. It was the foundation for everything else. Differently: deal with the identified warehouse copies on day one. They were the largest risk and the easiest to remove. Related: a DPDP-ready consent platform for lending, a FHIR and OMOP evidence platform, India's proposed frontier AI law and the India page.

FAQ

Common questions

When do the DPDP Rules apply?

In phases. The Rules were notified in November 2025. The consent manager framework applies from 13 November 2026, and most obligations on data fiduciaries, including notices, consent, rights, breach reporting and children's data, from 13 May 2027, according to published timelines.

How does ABDM fit with DPDP consent?

ABDM has its own consent flow for sharing health records between providers through a patient's health account. A provider integrating with ABDM still has its own DPDP duties for the data it holds, so both need to be designed together.

Can a diagnostics company use patient data for research?

Only on a sound legal basis and with appropriate safeguards. In this design, research uses pseudonymised data in a separate store, with purposes recorded and consent captured where it is the basis.
