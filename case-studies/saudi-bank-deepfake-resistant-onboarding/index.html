Raising the cost of a fake face: deepfake-resistant customer onboarding for a Saudi digital bank

A Saudi digital bank onboarded customers entirely through its app, using a selfie video matched to an ID document. Deepfake tools were making that check cheaper to beat. The goal was to raise the cost of a fake identity well above the cost of the defences, without making genuine customers suffer.

The situation

A digital bank in Saudi Arabia opened accounts entirely in its app: the customer photographed their ID, recorded a short selfie video, and a vendor's model compared the two. It worked well for genuine customers. Fraud teams were seeing more attempts that looked like synthetic faces and replayed or injected video, and the existing check had one weak point: if the video was convincing, the account opened.

Why now. On 15 October 2026, SDAIA and the Communications, Space and Technology Commission launched the "It's AI" campaign on the risks of deepfakes, including fraud and identity theft, as part of Saudi Arabia's Year of AI1. The CST's guidance lists countermeasures including labelling AI-generated content, detection tools and international provenance standards such as C2PA2. For a bank whose front door is a camera, the message was clear: assume faces and voices can be faked.

What had to be true

  • No single check decides: several independent signals combine into a risk score.
  • Trust the capture, not just the content: prove video came from a genuine camera on a genuine device.
  • Genuine customers barely notice; friction rises only with risk.
  • Personal and biometric data handled under the PDPL, processed in the Kingdom and kept briefly34.
  • People in the loop for the hard cases, with good tools and guidance.

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: attacks that never touch the camera

The most dangerous attacks skip the camera altogether, feeding generated or replayed video into a tampered app or emulator. Better face models do not help if the input is fake. The answer was to raise the attacker's cost at the capture step: device and app attestation, plus camera integrity checks, so injected video fails before any face comparison runs. The same reasoning appears in security design elsewhere: in the Model Context Protocol's analysis of client impersonation, adding attestation moves an attack from cheap to costly6.

Do not try to out-detect every deepfake. Make faking the capture expensive, and combine enough independent signals that no single fake is enough.

Where documents or media carried content credentials, the platform recorded them as one more signal; provenance standards such as C2PA are designed to show how content was created and edited5. Their absence was never treated as proof of fraud. Related reading: defender-first AI and patch cycles and detecting fraud rings with a knowledge graph.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: red-team the onboarding flow before buying anything; it showed the weak point was capture, not matching. Differently: build reviewer tooling earlier. The quality of human decisions on hard cases depended more on the tools than on the guidance. See also Saudi PDPL and cross-border AI.

FAQ

Common questions

Can liveness detection alone stop deepfakes?

No single check is enough. Attackers can inject pre-recorded or generated video directly into the app, bypassing the camera, so liveness should be combined with device and camera integrity, document checks and behavioural risk signals.

What is device attestation in onboarding?

A way for the app to prove it is running unmodified on a genuine device, so the bank can trust that video and images came from the real camera rather than being injected by an emulator or tampered app.

How does Saudi data protection law affect biometric onboarding?

Biometric and identity data are personal data under the PDPL, and transfers outside the Kingdom need an appropriate basis and safeguards. Processing in the Kingdom and keeping retention short simplifies compliance; legal interpretation stays with counsel.
