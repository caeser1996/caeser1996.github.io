When the suite goes down: keeping a Singapore digital insurer running through a productivity SaaS outage

A Singapore digital insurer ran its customer-facing platforms on its own cloud estate, with careful failover. Everything around them, email, chat, documents, identity and increasingly AI assistants, ran on one productivity suite. The insurer had planned for its own systems failing, not for that suite failing. A long outage at a major provider made the gap obvious, and Singapore's business continuity expectations for financial institutions made it urgent.

The situation

A Singapore digital insurer sold travel, health and home policies online. Its policy administration, claims and payments ran on its own cloud estate across two availability zones, with tested failover. Everything else ran on one productivity suite: email, chat, video calls, shared documents, the identity provider staff used to sign in to every other system, and the AI assistant built into it. Customer notices, incident bridges and even the runbooks for failing over the core platform lived there.

MAS's business continuity management guidelines expect financial institutions to identify their critical business services and set, for each, a service recovery time objective: how long it can be disrupted before severe impact, with the recovery times of underlying functions and dependencies fitting inside it23. The insurer had done this for its platforms. It had not traced the dependencies through the productivity suite.

Why now. On 12 November 2026, a long Microsoft 365 outage disrupted Outlook, Exchange Online and Teams for much of a working day; Microsoft attributed it to servers failing health checks and dropping out of service, with no evidence of an attack1. Staff across the industry found they could not email customers, join incident calls or open runbooks. The insurer's board asked a simple question: what would we have done?

What had to be true

  • Dependencies known: each critical service mapped to the SaaS it relies on, identity included.
  • Targets met: workarounds that keep each service within its recovery target.
  • Coordination possible: a way to run an incident without email, chat or shared documents.
  • Customers reachable: notices that do not depend on the failed suite.
  • Access preserved: administrators can reach critical systems if the identity provider is down.
  • Early warning: the insurer knows about an outage before staff start complaining.

Architecture

The first deliverable was a dependency map for each critical business service, built with the people who run them. It showed that the identity provider was the biggest single point of failure: if staff could not sign in, they could not reach the claims system, the cloud console or the paging tool, even though those were healthy.

The data flow, end to end

Detection used the OpenTelemetry Collector's HTTP check receiver, run from small collectors in more than one region, to probe the suite's sign-in, mail and chat endpoints alongside the insurer's own services4. Results went to the observability platform the insurer already used, which did not depend on the suite.

The hard part: identity and the fallback nobody uses

Break-glass without a back door. Emergency accounts that bypass the main identity provider are exactly what attackers want. The insurer kept a small number, each tied to a named role, protected with hardware security keys, stored with sealed procedures, monitored for any use and tested on a schedule. Any use outside an exercise raised an immediate alert to security.

Keeping fallbacks alive. A fallback channel that nobody opens for months will not work when needed: accounts expire, numbers change, people forget. The insurer used the out-of-band messaging tool for real on-call handovers every week, synchronised its contact lists from the HR system daily, and printed a short card of first steps for on-call staff.

Regulatory duties without email. Incident reporting to MAS and notices to customers normally went out by email. The runbook named an alternative route for each, and the compliance team rehearsed it, so a suite outage would not also become a reporting failure.

Resilience plans usually cover your systems failing. The outage that hurts is often the supplier your plan quietly depends on: the one hosting the plan itself.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: map dependencies service by service with the people who run them; it surfaced the identity dependency nobody had written down. Differently: include the AI assistant in the map from the start; some teams had begun relying on it for routine customer replies, and its outage was a gap in its own right. FAQ

Common questions

Why plan for a productivity suite outage?

Email, chat, documents, identity and AI assistants often all depend on one provider. When it fails, incident coordination, customer communication and access to other systems can fail with it, even if your own platforms are healthy.

What are service recovery time objectives in MAS's guidelines?

MAS's business continuity guidelines expect financial institutions to set, for each critical business service, the time within which it must be recovered before severe impact, and to make sure the recovery times of underlying functions and dependencies fit within it.

What is break-glass access?

A small number of tightly controlled emergency accounts that let administrators reach critical systems when the normal sign-in route, such as the main identity provider, is unavailable. They are monitored, tested and used only in emergencies.
