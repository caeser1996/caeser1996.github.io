Giving a fast-moving seed team the technical leadership investors expect at Series A

Founders shipping quickly, customers arriving, and a Series A process ahead. Architecture decisions lived in chat threads, security questionnaires were piling up and nobody owned hiring for senior engineers. The goal was technical leadership that fitted the stage: enough structure to scale and pass scrutiny, without slowing the team down.

The situation

A seed-stage B2B software company had found traction. The founders were strong builders and the small engineering team shipped quickly, but the things that matter at the next stage were falling behind. Architecture choices were made in chat and forgotten, the release process depended on one person, there was no shared view of reliability, and enterprise prospects were sending security questionnaires nobody had time to answer well. A Series A process was on the horizon, and technical diligence would follow.

The company did not yet need, or want to pay for, a full-time CTO. It needed senior technical leadership for part of each week, with a clear plan for handing over.

What had to be true

  • A roadmap the team believed, connecting product goals to platform work and technical debt.
  • Decisions written down, so they can be reviewed, challenged and learned by new hires.
  • Delivery and reliability measured, as signals for improvement rather than targets to game.
  • Security that stands up to scrutiny, with controls and evidence rather than promises.
  • A team that outlasts the engagement: senior hires and habits that keep working after hand-off.

Architecture

Here the architecture was as much the operating model as the system. The technical work rested on a light set of leadership practices, each producing an artefact the team and investors could see.

Architecture decision records followed the lightweight format Michael Nygard described: context, decision and consequences, kept with the code1. How I use them in a fractional role is in architecture decision records for fractional CTOs.

The data flow, end to end

The cadence mattered more than any single document. Each cycle followed the same loop:

Delivery was tracked with DORA's software delivery metrics, which cover throughput and instability. DORA itself warns against turning them into blanket targets, so they were used per service and only to find bottlenecks2. See DORA metrics for AI-assisted engineering. Reliability used a small number of SLOs on the journeys customers cared about, with an agreed policy for what happens when an error budget is spent3.

The hard part: adding structure without slowing the team

A fast seed team is right to be suspicious of process. Every practice had to earn its place by saving more time than it cost. That meant:

  • ADRs only for decisions that are expensive to reverse, written in under an hour, not for every library choice.
  • Automating the evidence: delivery metrics from the pipeline, access reviews from the identity provider, change history from pull requests.
  • Sequencing security work against real demand. The SOC 2 trust services criteria (security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy) gave the structure4, with security controls first because every enterprise questionnaire asked about them. More in SOC 2 for AI startups.
  • Hiring senior engineers who could own areas, not just add capacity, so leadership could be handed over rather than borrowed.

A fractional CTO succeeds when the role becomes unnecessary. The test is whether decisions, metrics and hiring keep happening well in the weeks you are not there.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: write decisions down from the first week and keep the cadence predictable, even when the week is busy. Differently: start the search for the permanent engineering leader earlier. Good senior hires take longer than any technical workstream, and the hand-off is only as strong as the person receiving it.

FAQ

Common questions

When does a startup need a fractional CTO?

Typically when the product is gaining customers, technical decisions start to carry long-term cost, and investors or enterprise buyers begin asking questions about architecture, security and the team that the founders cannot yet answer with evidence.

How does a fractional CTO work with a full-time team?

Through a fixed cadence: regular architecture and delivery reviews, written decision records, and direct involvement in hiring. The aim is to build habits and people that keep working when the fractional role ends.

Does a seed-stage startup need SOC 2?

Not always, but if enterprise customers are in the plan, building the controls and evidence early is cheaper than retrofitting them. The trust services criteria give a clear structure to start from.
