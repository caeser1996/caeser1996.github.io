Making a decades-old media archive findable with model enrichment and a controlled vocabulary

A large archive of video, audio and photographs, catalogued under decades of changing conventions, with most items described by little more than a title and a date. The goal was consistent, searchable metadata at archive scale, without letting a model invent the catalogue.

The situation

A media organisation held decades of video, audio and photography. The material was valuable for new programmes, licensing and research, but most of it was hard to find. Cataloguing conventions had changed several times, many items carried only a title and a broadcast date, and the same subject was described in a dozen ways. Search worked if you already knew the exact words a cataloguer had used years earlier, and otherwise depended on a few long-serving people who remembered where things were.

The organisation wanted to use AI to describe the backlog. The concern, rightly, was that a model left to itself would produce confident, inconsistent tags at a scale nobody could review.

What had to be true

  • One controlled vocabulary, so a subject, place or person is described the same way across the archive.
  • Machine suggestions, human authority: models propose, people decide where confidence is low or the subject is sensitive.
  • Provenance on every field, so anyone can see whether a label came from a cataloguer, a model or a reviewer.
  • Search by meaning and by exact term, for editors who know what they want and for those who are exploring.
  • A taxonomy the archive team owns, not one hidden inside prompts or code.

Architecture

The vocabulary was modelled as a SKOS concept scheme, which gives each concept a preferred label, alternative labels and broader or narrower relationships1. Descriptive fields such as creator, date, subject and rights were aligned with DCMI Metadata Terms so that records stayed portable between systems2.

The data flow, end to end

For semantic search, transcripts and summaries were embedded with a sentence-embedding model, which produces vectors that can be compared with cosine similarity3. Nearest-neighbour lookup used an HNSW index, a graph-based approximate search method that scales well to large collections4. Model choice and storage trade-offs are covered in choosing embedding models and pgvector or a vector database.

The hard part: a vocabulary people agree on

Running models over the archive was the easy part. Agreeing what the labels should be took far longer. The approach that worked:

  • Seed the concept scheme from the terms cataloguers already used most, then merge duplicates as alternative labels rather than deleting them, so old records still match.
  • Use SKOS mapping properties to link local concepts to external vocabularies where they existed, instead of copying those vocabularies wholesale1.
  • Never let the pipeline create a concept. A model suggestion with no match becomes a proposal for the taxonomy owner, with example items attached.
  • Treat people and sensitive topics as always-reviewed fields, whatever the model's confidence.

The taxonomy design choices are discussed further in SKOS vs OWL for enterprise taxonomies.

A controlled vocabulary turns a model from an author into an assistant. The model can only choose from concepts people have agreed, and everything else becomes a question for a person.

How the engagement ran

Thresholds were set against a hand-labelled evaluation set drawn from the pilot collection, not against the model's own sense of certainty. The method is in LLM evaluation sets that predict production.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: put the vocabulary at the centre and make provenance a field, not a log file. Differently: involve the cataloguers in designing the review screen from the first week. The speed and quality of review decided the throughput of the whole system, far more than the model did.

FAQ

Common questions

Can a language model tag an archive on its own?

It can suggest tags quickly, but unreviewed free-text tags drift and duplicate. Mapping suggestions onto a controlled vocabulary and routing low-confidence items to people keeps the catalogue consistent and trustworthy.

Why use SKOS for a media taxonomy?

SKOS is a W3C standard for thesauri and taxonomies. It gives preferred and alternative labels, broader and narrower relationships and mappings to external vocabularies, in a form that tools and search engines can reuse.

Do you still need keyword search if you have embeddings?

Yes. Embeddings find items by meaning, but editors still search for exact names, dates and identifiers. A hybrid of filtered keyword search and vector similarity serves both needs.
