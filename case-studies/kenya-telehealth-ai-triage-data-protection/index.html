Helpful, not reckless: privacy-first AI triage for a Kenyan telehealth service

A Kenyan telehealth service wanted an AI assistant to take symptoms over chat, in English and Swahili, and route patients to the right clinician faster. Health data is the most sensitive data it holds, the best models run offshore, and Kenya's regulator is moving from warnings to enforcement. The design had to keep patients safe, keep identifiable data at home and prove both.

The situation

A telehealth provider in Kenya offered consultations by app, WhatsApp and SMS, backed by a network of clinicians and partner pharmacies. Demand peaked in the evenings, when patients waited longest for a first response. The team wanted an AI assistant to take a structured symptom history in English or Swahili, summarise it and suggest who should see the patient, so clinicians could start from a clear picture.

Why now. Kenya's Data Protection Act treats health data as sensitive personal data and allows transfers abroad only with appropriate safeguards1. The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC) consulted in 2026 on draft guidance on AI2, and commentary on the draft says uses such as health diagnostics and clinical decision support would need a data protection impact assessment3. There were also public calls for stronger protection of data sent to offshore AI providers4, and an October 2026 report described African regulators, Kenya's among them, using data protection law to police AI while dedicated AI bills stall5. A dedicated AI law may or may not arrive soon. The data protection duties already apply.

What had to be true

  • Safety first: urgent symptoms escalated by rules, never left to a model's judgement.
  • Clinician in the loop: the AI drafts, a clinician decides.
  • Identifiable data stays home: names, numbers and IDs never reach an offshore model.
  • Both languages work, including mixed English and Swahili, tested on every change.
  • A living DPIA, updated with each release and backed by evidence.

Architecture

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: giving the model context without giving it identities

Good triage needs context: age band, past visits, current medicines, allergies. Shipping the whole record to the model would have been easy and wrong. The team instead exposed clinic systems through small MCP servers running in-country, each offering narrow, read-only tools such as "medicines for this patient token" or "visits in the last 90 days"6. The servers return only the fields the tool needs, keyed by token, so the model can reason about the patient without ever seeing who they are.

De-identification in free text is harder than in records. Patients type names, phone numbers, locations and the names of relatives, often mixing English and Swahili. The gateway combined pattern rules with a small local model for entity detection, tested on real, consented examples in both languages, and failed closed: if it was unsure, the message went to a human rather than to the model. More on securing tool servers in the MCP security checklist.

The question is not "can we send this to the model?" but "what is the least the model needs to be useful?" Build tools that answer exactly that.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: shadow mode. It showed where the model asked poor follow-up questions in Swahili before any patient saw them. Differently: build the bilingual evaluation set first, not alongside the gateway. It was the slowest part to get right and it drove most design choices. Related: private AI for clinical documentation, private LLMs, evaluation sets, healthcare data architecture and where I work.

FAQ

Common questions

Does Kenya regulate AI in healthcare?

Kenya has no AI-specific law in force. The Data Protection Act 2019 applies, with health data treated as sensitive personal data. The ODPC has consulted on draft guidance on AI, and commentary on that draft says health diagnostics and clinical decision support would need a data protection impact assessment.

Can a Kenyan provider send patient data to an offshore AI model?

Cross-border transfers are allowed only with appropriate safeguards under the Data Protection Act, and there is growing public pressure for stronger protections when data goes to offshore AI providers. Minimising and de-identifying data before it leaves the country reduces both legal and practical risk.

Should an AI assistant make triage decisions on its own?

Not in this design. Rules catch red-flag symptoms first, the model drafts a summary and suggested routing, and a clinician confirms before it affects care.
