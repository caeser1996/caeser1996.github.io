Agents inside, attackers outside: AI cyber risk controls for a UK payments firm

A UK payments firm had AI agents in customer support, fraud operations and engineering, each built differently and each with its own access. At the same time, attacks on it were getting faster and more convincing. The goal was one way to build and govern agents, and controls that assume attackers use AI too, in a form the firm could show its regulators.

The situation

A UK payments and e-money firm had adopted AI quickly and unevenly. Customer support used an agent that could look up transactions and raise refunds. Fraud operations used one that summarised alerts and suggested account restrictions. Engineering used coding agents with access to repositories and deployment pipelines. Each was built by a different team, on a different framework, with its own service accounts and its own idea of logging.

At the same time, the attacks the firm saw were changing: more convincing phishing, impersonation of executives in calls and messages asking for urgent payment changes, and faster exploitation of newly published flaws. The firm already worked to the FCA's operational resilience rules, which require important business services to be identified and kept within impact tolerances3.

Why now. In November 2026 the Bank of England, PRA and FCA published a consultation on cyber risk and resilience management, CP17/26 and GC26/9, clarifying how firms should meet existing requirements, with responses due by 26 February 20272. Alongside it they published effective practices on AI-related cyber risk, which treat AI as creating new risks, amplifying existing ones and helping defenders, and which include reducing the attack surface against AI-enabled attacks1. The board wanted to know how the firm compared, before a supervisor asked.

What had to be true

  • Every agent known: owner, purpose, model, tools and data it can reach.
  • One way to build agents, so controls are written once.
  • Agent actions as accountable as staff actions: identity, least privilege, logs and a way to stop them.
  • Defences that assume attackers use AI, especially for payments and access changes.
  • Everything tied to important business services, so AI risk fits the resilience framework the firm already runs.

The hard part: controlling agents the firm did not build the same way

The three agent estates could not be governed one by one; there was no shared place to put a control. The answer was to move them onto one framework over time, so identity, tool access, logging and a kill switch were properties of the platform rather than of each team's code. OpenTelemetry's work on agent observability draws the same distinction between agent applications and the frameworks they are built on, and argues for standard telemetry at the framework level so every application inherits it5.

The fastest risk reduction came before any migration: an inventory, then removing tools agents did not need. Some had write access they had never used.

The second half of the hard part was outside the firm. Impersonation attacks target the same processes agents touch: refunds, payee changes, admin access. Those changes now need a person to approve them through a separate, verified channel, never on the strength of a message, call or agent request alone. The pattern follows the deepfake-resistant onboarding and staff access hardening case studies, and the agent platform builds on the enterprise MCP agent platform.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: start with the inventory and the tool gateway, which reduced risk before any larger change, and treat the regulators' effective practices as a checklist rather than waiting for final guidance. Differently: involve the contact centre and finance teams from day one. The impersonation attempts reached them first, and their ideas made the approval steps practical. The NCSC's Cyber Assessment Framework, which the consultation aligns with4, was a useful common language between engineering and risk. FAQ

Common questions

What did UK regulators publish on AI cyber risk?

In November 2026 the Bank of England, PRA and FCA published a consultation on cyber risk and resilience management, CP17/26 and GC26/9, and separate effective practices describing how firms are managing AI-related cyber risk, including reducing their attack surface against AI-enabled attacks.

Are effective practices mandatory?

No. They describe practices regulators consider effective. In practice they become a reference point in supervisory discussions, so firms should know how their own controls compare.

Why standardise on one agent framework?

Controls such as identity, tool access, logging and kill switches can then be built once and applied to every agent, instead of being re-implemented, or forgotten, in each team's own build.
