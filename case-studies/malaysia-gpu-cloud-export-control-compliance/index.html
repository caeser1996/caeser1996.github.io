Know your compute customer: building export-control compliance into a Malaysian GPU cloud

A Malaysian data centre operator had added a GPU cloud to serve regional AI start-ups, enterprises and research labs. Demand was strong. So was scrutiny: Malaysia had introduced permits for moving US-origin AI chips, and in November 2026 the US moved to require licences for advanced chips going to Malaysia and four other countries, including to stop restricted parties reaching them remotely. The operator needed to prove, continuously, who was using its GPUs and for what, without becoming impossible to buy from.

The situation

A Malaysian data centre operator added a GPU cloud to its colocation business, offering training and inference capacity to start-ups, enterprises and university labs across South-East Asia. Customers signed up online, were given a Kubernetes namespace and GPU quota, and could start work within a day. Onboarding checked payment details and little else.

Malaysia had already tightened its own controls: since July 2025, exporting, transshipping or moving US-origin high-performance AI chips through the country requires a permit under the Strategic Trade Act34. The operator's hardware stayed in its own halls, so that rule mostly affected its suppliers. The bigger question was who was using the hardware remotely.

Why now. In November 2026, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued a rule requiring licences for advanced AI chips going to Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, and aimed at stopping restricted parties from reaching controlled chips through data centres in third countries12. For a GPU cloud in Malaysia, that made its customer list part of its supply chain: future hardware purchases would depend on being able to show where compute went.

What had to be true

  • Known customers: every tenant screened, including who ultimately owns or controls it.
  • Gated access: no GPU capacity until checks are complete, and access paused if they lapse.
  • Known users: every person and service using a tenant's GPUs identifiable.
  • Traceable usage: who used which GPUs, when and from where, kept as evidence.
  • Watchful: unusual patterns surfaced for review.
  • Still easy to buy: legitimate customers onboard in days, not months.

Architecture

The platform already used Kueue to queue and admit GPU jobs fairly across tenants. That admission step became the enforcement point. Each tenant's queue was linked to its compliance status: if screening was incomplete, had expired or was under review, the tenant's jobs stayed suspended rather than running. Kueue's model of jobs waiting in a queue until admitted made this a configuration change rather than a new system5.

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: remote access and changing customers

Who is really using the GPUs? A screened customer can resell capacity, share credentials or be acquired. The operator required named user identities with multi-factor sign-in, prohibited credential sharing and resale without approval in its terms, and logged where each session came from. Monitoring looked for patterns worth a human look: many users appearing under one small tenant, access from unexpected locations, or workloads that did not match the declared use.

Rescreening without friction. Ownership and restricted-party lists change. Rather than rescreen everyone at once, the platform rescreened tenants on a schedule and whenever lists were updated or a tenant's details changed, pausing only those with new matches for review. Legitimate customers rarely noticed.

Not a law firm. Export-control questions often need legal judgement. The platform's job was to collect facts and enforce decisions consistently; edge cases went to compliance staff and external counsel, and their decisions were recorded alongside the evidence.

For a GPU cloud, the customer list is now part of the hardware supply chain. Build compliance into the scheduler, not a spreadsheet, so it cannot be bypassed on a busy day.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: put enforcement in the admission step of the scheduler; it made compliance consistent without slowing engineers. Differently: involve the hardware suppliers earlier, since their own export obligations shaped what evidence they needed from the operator. FAQ

Common questions

What changed for GPU clouds in Malaysia in November 2026?

Reporting says the US Commerce Department issued a rule requiring licences for advanced AI chips going to Malaysia and four other countries and targeting remote access to controlled chips through data centres in third countries. This adds to Malaysia's 2025 requirement for permits to export, transship or transit US-origin high-performance AI chips.

What is know-your-customer for compute?

Screening and verifying who uses compute capacity, including who ultimately owns or controls the customer, and keeping that knowledge current as customers, users and workloads change.

How can a Kubernetes platform enforce compliance for GPU access?

By tying GPU quota and job admission to each tenant's compliance status, so jobs from tenants whose checks are incomplete or have lapsed wait or are refused, and by logging every job and access with the identity behind it.
