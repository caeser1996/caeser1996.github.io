Connecting maintenance history, sensors and drawings so engineers can find root causes faster

Maintenance history in one system, sensor data in another, drawings and procedures in a document store. Every root-cause investigation started with an engineer stitching them together by hand. The goal was one asset knowledge graph that linked them, with an assistant that answers questions and shows its working.

The situation

An energy operator ran a large estate of rotating equipment, vessels, pipework and instrumentation. When something failed, the reliability team asked the obvious questions: has this happened before, on this unit or on similar ones, what did the sensors show beforehand, and what did we change last time? Answering meant searching the maintenance system, exporting historian trends, finding the right revision of a drawing and reading free-text work order notes. Each investigation rebuilt the same picture from scratch.

The data existed. The connections between it did not.

What had to be true

  • One identity per asset, reconciled across the register, the maintenance system, tag lists and documents.
  • A shared vocabulary for equipment classes, failure modes and maintenance actions, so "similar assets" and "same failure" mean something consistent.
  • Systems of record untouched: link to them, don't replace them.
  • Answers with evidence, because engineers will not act on an unexplained answer.
  • Owned by the business: reliability engineers, not just the data team, maintain the model.

Architecture

The ontology borrowed its structure from ISO 14224, which organises reliability and maintenance data into equipment, failure and maintenance categories with a defined equipment taxonomy1. Sensor concepts followed the W3C and OGC Semantic Sensor Network ontology, with SOSA as its lightweight core2, rather than inventing our own model of observations.

Curated tables sat in Apache Iceberg, whose snapshot-based metadata gives versioned, reproducible inputs to every graph build5. See lakehouse catalog strategy and ontology vs knowledge graph vs semantic layer.

The data flow, end to end

SHACL let us express the rules ("every pump has exactly one parent system", "every work order references a resolved asset") as shapes that are validated on every build3. More in SHACL validation for knowledge graphs.

The hard part: deciding that two things are the same asset

Entity resolution took more effort than the graph itself. The same pump appeared under a tag number in the register, a slightly different tag in the maintenance system, a description in free-text notes and a drawing number in the document store. Some tags had been reused after equipment was replaced.

  • Deterministic rules first (exact tag matches within a site), probabilistic matching second.
  • Every match carries a confidence and a method, so low-confidence links are visible, not hidden.
  • A review queue where reliability engineers confirm or reject uncertain matches; their decisions train the rules.
  • Equipment replacement modelled explicitly, so history follows the functional location and the physical unit separately.

The patterns are described in entity resolution for knowledge graphs.

A knowledge graph is only as good as its identities. Spend the first weeks on what counts as "the same thing", not on the database.

How the engagement ran

Key decisions and trade-offs

Align to ISO 14224 and SOSA/SSNBespoke modelRecognised structure; easier to extend and explain
Pointers to time seriesCopy readings into the graphGraphs are poor at high-volume time series

The assistant combined graph traversal with document retrieval. GraphRAG research shows graph-based indexes help with broad, corpus-level questions that plain vector retrieval handles poorly4; here the graph already existed, so retrieval followed its edges. See when GraphRAG beats vector RAG.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: write competency questions with engineers before modelling anything, and make every link explainable. Differently: put the review queue in front of engineers sooner. Their corrections improved resolution more than any tuning we did.

FAQ

Common questions

Why use a knowledge graph for asset data?

Asset questions are about relationships: which components sit in which system, which work orders touched them, which sensors watch them and which drawings describe them. A graph stores those relationships directly, so they can be queried and traversed instead of rebuilt by hand.

Is a knowledge graph a replacement for the CMMS or historian?

No. The systems of record stay where they are. The graph holds identifiers, relationships and summaries, and links back to the source systems for detail and high-volume time series.

Where does an LLM fit?

As an interface. The assistant translates questions into graph and time series queries, then answers with citations to the records it used, so engineers can check the evidence.
