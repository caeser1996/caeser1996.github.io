Fast, lawful and reviewable: creditworthiness assessment at checkout for an EU buy now, pay later provider

A buy now, pay later provider operating in several EU countries approved purchases at checkout with a light-touch risk check. The revised Consumer Credit Directive brings that kind of credit into scope from 20 November 2026, with a proper creditworthiness assessment, limits on the data that may be used and a right to human review of automated decisions. The goal was to meet all three without making checkout slow.

The situation

A buy now, pay later provider operated in several EU countries, approving split payments at online checkouts. Its risk check was built for speed and fraud: device signals, basket details, repayment history with the provider and a fraud model. It was not designed as a creditworthiness assessment, and it used whatever data improved the model.

The revised Consumer Credit Directive changes that. Member States had to apply it from 20 November 20261, and it brings much buy now, pay later lending into scope as regulated consumer credit2. That means a creditworthiness assessment based on relevant information, limits on what personal data may be used, and, where the assessment involves automated processing, a right for the consumer to obtain human intervention, an explanation and a review13.

Why now. The rules applied from 20 November 2026, while several large markets were still finishing their national laws2. The provider needed one platform that met the directive everywhere it operated and could absorb national differences as each country's law settled4.

What had to be true

  • A real creditworthiness assessment for every in-scope agreement, not a relabelled fraud score.
  • Only permitted data in decisions, enforced by the platform, not by convention.
  • Checkout stays fast: the decision has to fit inside the merchant's payment flow.
  • Every automated decision reviewable: what was used, which rules and model, and why.
  • National differences as configuration, because each country's law arrived on its own timetable.

The hard part: keeping prohibited data out of decisions, and out of logs

The old model had learned from everything available. Some of those signals were reasonable for fraud but inappropriate for creditworthiness, and some could stand in for categories of data the rules exclude. The fix was to tag every feature in the feature store with where it may be used: fraud only, credit permitted, or not used, per country. The decision service could only request credit features from the permitted set, so a model trained on something else simply could not be deployed for credit.

Logs were the second leak. Request headers and payloads carried details that should never reach a credit decision or a debugging tool. The team set telemetry to exclude by default and include only what was needed, masking sensitive values. OpenTelemetry's eBPF instrumentation shows the pattern, with a default exclude policy and an obfuscation value for headers that must be visible but not readable5.

Data restrictions only work if they are enforced where features are served. A policy document cannot stop a data scientist from adding a column; a permission on the feature can.

The decision record and review workbench reused the approach from the explainable credit decisions case study, with one difference: here the record had to be written within the checkout budget, so it was designed as a single append to a fast store with asynchronous enrichment afterwards. Idempotency followed idempotent payment pipelines, so retries from merchants never produced two decisions.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: treat the permitted data list as a product owned jointly by credit risk and engineering, enforced in the feature store. Differently: involve the review team before building the workbench. They knew which context they needed to decide quickly and fairly, and the first version showed too much detail in the wrong order. See structured outputs for how explanations were generated consistently and the EU AI Act roadmap for the high-risk credit scoring rules that come later.

FAQ

Common questions

When does the revised Consumer Credit Directive apply?

Member States had to transpose Directive (EU) 2023/2225 by 20 November 2025 and apply it from 20 November 2026. Agreements already in place on that date generally stay under the old rules, and national laws differ in detail.

Does it cover buy now, pay later?

Yes, much of it. Deferred payment arrangements where a third party provides the credit are brought into scope, which means a creditworthiness assessment and the directive's other consumer protections apply.

What does it say about automated decisions?

Where a creditworthiness assessment involves automated processing, the consumer can request human intervention, an explanation of the assessment and a review of the decision, and can express their point of view.
