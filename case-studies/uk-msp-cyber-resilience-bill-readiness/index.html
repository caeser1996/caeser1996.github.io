Ready before it is law: preparing a UK managed service provider for the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill

A mid-sized UK managed service provider held administrator access to hundreds of customer environments. The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would bring providers like it under the NIS regulations for the first time. The goal was to get ahead of it: control privileged access centrally, detect and report incidents quickly, and tell affected customers promptly.

The situation

A UK managed service provider looked after IT for several hundred organisations: small businesses, schools, professional services firms and a few public-sector suppliers. Its technicians held administrator access to customer networks, cloud tenants and line-of-business systems. Over the years, access had accumulated in shared accounts, remote management tools and scripts with stored credentials.

Why now. The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would bring medium and large managed service providers within the scope of the NIS regulations, because of the privileged access they hold to customer systems12. It also proposes duties to report significant incidents and to identify and notify customers likely to be affected3. In October 2026 the Bill was still before Parliament4, but the direction was clear, and customers were already asking about it in renewals.

What had to be true

  • No standing administrator access to customer environments.
  • Every privileged session attributable to a named technician, approved and recorded.
  • Incidents detected and escalated fast, with a process that can support early notification and a fuller report.
  • Affected customers identifiable within minutes, not days of spreadsheet work.
  • Automation under the same rules as people, including AI-assisted tools.

The hard part: one front door for hundreds of environments

Access was spread across per-customer accounts in dozens of tools, each with its own login and its own way of revoking access. The design moved to a single identity provider for all technician and automation access, with customers' systems trusting it for scoped, time-limited sessions. The same principle is spreading in AI tooling: the Model Context Protocol's Enterprise-Managed Authorization lets an organisation provision access to many servers through its identity provider, scoped to users' groups and roles, instead of authenticating to each one separately6.

An MSP's biggest risk is its own access. Make every privileged session temporary, attributable and recorded, and most of the regulatory work follows.

Automation got no exemption. Scripts and AI-assisted tools used scoped service identities through the same broker, with no stored administrator passwords. More on that in securing MCP servers and sandboxing AI agents.

How it ran

Controls were mapped to the NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework, which organises security into outcomes for managing risk, protecting against attack, detecting events and minimising impact5. That gave customers and, later, a regulator a recognised way to read the programme.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: start with the privileged access inventory, because it surfaced most of the risk. Differently: run the first notification drill earlier. It showed that knowing which customers were affected was harder than detecting the incident. Related reading: defender-first AI and patch cycles and an AI copilot for the security operations centre.

FAQ

Common questions

Will the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill apply to managed service providers?

As introduced, the Bill brings medium and large managed service providers within the scope of the NIS regulations, because of their privileged access to customer systems; small and micro businesses are excluded. It was still before Parliament at the time of writing, so check the final text.

What should an MSP do before the Bill becomes law?

Remove standing privileged access, route technician access through a central identity provider with time limits and recording, build an incident process that can notify quickly, and be able to identify which customers an incident affects.

How does the NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework help?

It gives a structured set of outcomes for managing security risk, protecting against attack, detecting events and minimising impact. Mapping controls to it gives a clear, recognised picture of readiness.
