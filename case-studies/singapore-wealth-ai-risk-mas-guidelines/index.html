From guidelines to plumbing: putting MAS's AI risk management guidelines to work at a Singapore wealth manager

A Singapore wealth manager had AI everywhere and an inventory nowhere: vendor copilots, a suitability model, generative AI drafting client letters, and agents piloted by operations. MAS's final guidelines on AI risk management made the board accountable for all of it. The task was to turn the guidelines into systems that keep the inventory, the controls and the evidence current without a spreadsheet army.

The situation

A Singapore-based wealth manager served private and affluent clients across Asia. AI had arrived from every direction: a suitability scoring model built in-house, a vendor's generative AI assistant for relationship managers, a document tool that drafted client letters, transaction monitoring with machine learning inside, and an operations pilot using AI agents to reconcile statements. Risk kept a short spreadsheet of "AI models". Nobody believed it was complete.

Why now. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) issued its final Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management on 7 October 202613. Published summaries describe guidelines that cover all forms of AI, including generative AI and agents, on a risk-proportionate basis; that put boards and senior management in charge of AI risk appetite and oversight; and that keep the institution accountable for AI bought from vendors23. Commentary reports transition periods into 2027 and 20283. The authoritative text sits with MAS4. The board wanted to know what the firm actually used, how risky each use was, and how it would know when something went wrong.

What had to be true

  • A complete, living inventory of AI, built and bought, including features inside vendor products.
  • Risk tiers that change the controls: light for low-risk uses, rigorous for client-impacting ones.
  • Vendor AI held to the same bar, with evidence proportionate to risk.
  • Gates in the lifecycle: material AI cannot go live without evaluations and monitoring.
  • Board reporting from live data, not quarterly heroics.

The hard part: finding the AI you did not build

Most of the firm's AI was inside products it had bought. Vendors had switched on generative features in CRM, document and communications tools, often by default. Interviews missed them. The team used three nets: every generative AI call from internal systems went through an LLM gateway, which logged the application and model; procurement and renewals now asked vendors to declare AI features and their data use; and a periodic scan looked for AI SDKs and API keys in code and configuration. Each find created a draft inventory entry for an owner to complete.

Monitoring had a consistency problem. Each tool reported AI activity differently, so dashboards were stitched together by hand. The team adopted the OpenTelemetry generative AI conventions as the firm's telemetry contract and checked conformance in CI, so a service that emitted the wrong attributes failed its build rather than quietly breaking reports. OpenTelemetry Weaver provides exactly that kind of live check against a registry of conventions5.

Agents needed their own treatment. The reconciliation pilot was low risk in impact but high in autonomy, so its tier was raised: every action logged, narrow permissions and a human approval step for anything that changed client records. See when agents become the user and evaluating AI agents.

An AI inventory kept by survey is out of date the day it is finished. Wire discovery into the gateway, procurement and the codebase, and let people complete what the systems find.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: put the gateway in early. It quickly found AI uses the survey had missed. Differently: involve procurement from the start. Vendor features switched on at renewal were the largest source of new entries. FAQ

Common questions

What did MAS publish?

MAS issued its final Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management for financial institutions on 7 October 2026. According to published summaries, they apply to all forms of AI, including generative AI and AI agents, on a risk-proportionate basis, with board and senior management oversight.

Do the guidelines cover AI bought from vendors?

Yes. Commentary on the final guidelines says financial institutions keep primary accountability for third-party AI, and that independent assessments are preferred over vendor self-attestations where risk warrants.

When do firms need to comply?

Commentary describes transition periods running into 2027 and 2028 for different sections. Check the MAS text for the exact dates that apply to your institution.
