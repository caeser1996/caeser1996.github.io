Scaling a payments platform past a million transactions a day without double charges

As volume grew, retries and timeouts started producing duplicate charges and a growing manual reconciliation queue. The goal was a payment path that was safe to retry at every step, published events reliably, and reconciled itself, sized for more than a million transactions a day.

The situation

A payments platform processing card and bank transfers for merchants was growing quickly. The architecture had served it well at lower volume: a payments service writing to Postgres, calling external providers, then publishing an event for the ledger and notifications. As traffic climbed towards and past 1M+ transactions a day, the edges started to fray. Mobile clients retried on timeouts. Providers occasionally responded slowly and then succeeded anyway. Events were sometimes published for writes that rolled back, or not published for writes that committed.

The symptoms were familiar: occasional duplicate charges, refunds issued by hand, and a reconciliation team matching provider settlement files to internal records in spreadsheets.

What had to be true

  • Every step safe to retry, from the client to the provider call.
  • One source of truth per payment, with state changes that cannot be lost or invented.
  • Reliable events: published exactly when the database change commits.
  • A ledger that balances, and reconciliation that runs itself for the normal cases.
  • Operable by the in-house team, with clear signals when something drifts.

Architecture

The data flow, end to end

The edge followed the IETF Idempotency-Key header draft, which defines how clients make non-idempotent methods such as POST fault-tolerant1, and borrowed conventions familiar from established payment APIs, such as rejecting a reused key with different parameters2. The full pattern is in idempotent payments and order pipelines.

The hard part: the ambiguous middle

Duplicates at the edge are the easy case. The hard case is a request that times out somewhere in the middle: did the provider charge or not? The design handled it explicitly:

  • The payment state machine had an "unknown" state for ambiguous provider outcomes, rather than guessing success or failure.
  • Unknown payments were resolved by querying the provider, never by blindly retrying a charge.
  • Provider calls carried their own idempotency keys, so a retry that did happen could not create a second charge on the provider's side.
  • Events were produced through a transactional outbox: the change and the outbox record commit together and a relay publishes afterwards, which removes the dual-write problem without distributed transactions3. The relay used Debezium's outbox event router to turn outbox rows into routed Kafka events4.

Because the relay can deliver a message more than once, every consumer was idempotent too, keyed by event ID. CDC details are in change data capture and event streams.

Exactly-once is not a property you buy from a broker. It is what you get when every producer is reliable and every consumer is idempotent.

How the engagement ran

Every hop carried a trace context, so a single payment could be followed from the client request through the provider call, the outbox, the ledger posting and reconciliation when something looked wrong.

Service level objectives and alerting followed SLOs and error budgets for small teams.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: name the ambiguous states in the data model and design for them explicitly. Differently: build reconciliation earlier. It is the system that tells you whether everything else is working, and it is the first place a new failure mode shows up.

FAQ

Common questions

What causes double charges?

Usually a retry after an ambiguous failure: the client or a gateway times out, retries, and the original request had in fact succeeded. Without idempotency, the second request creates a second charge.

What is the transactional outbox pattern?

The service writes the business change and an outbox record in the same database transaction. A relay then publishes outbox records to the message broker, so events are sent if and only if the transaction commits.

Why automate reconciliation?

At high volume, manual matching of internal records to provider settlement files becomes a queue that never empties. Automated matching handles the routine cases and routes only genuine exceptions to people.
