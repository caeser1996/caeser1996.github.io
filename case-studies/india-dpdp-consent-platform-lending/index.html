Consent that actually propagates: preparing an Indian digital lender for the DPDP Rules

An Indian digital lender collected consent in its app but could not say, for any one customer, which partners held their data, for what purpose, or whether a withdrawal had reached them. With the DPDP Rules phasing in, the goal was a consent and data-rights platform that turns legal obligations into events every system honours.

The situation

A digital lender in India served customers through a mobile app, with credit decisions supported by bureaus, co-lending partners, verification vendors, a collections agency and marketing platforms. Consent was a set of checkboxes at sign-up. Personal data flowed to partners through file drops and APIs built over years by different teams, and nobody could answer a simple question for a single customer: who holds this person's data, for which purpose, and what happens if they withdraw?

Why now. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, bringing the DPDP Act, 2023 into force in stages over 18 months12. Registration of consent managers applies after the first year, which falls in November 2026, and most core obligations for data fiduciaries apply from around May 20273. For a lender whose business runs on personal and financial data, that is not much time to rebuild how consent works.

What had to be true

  • Consent per purpose, not one blanket checkbox, with the notice version recorded.
  • Withdrawal as easy as giving consent, and actually honoured by every system and partner.
  • Retention that deletes: data removed when its purpose ends, with evidence.
  • Breach detection and reporting fast enough to meet notification duties.
  • Room for consent managers, so customers can manage consent through a registered intermediary.

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: proving a withdrawal reached everyone

Recording consent is easy. Proving that a withdrawal stopped processing everywhere is not, because data has already been copied to analytics, partners and vendors. The design treated each withdrawal as an event on the stream, with every consumer and partner integration required to acknowledge it. Tracing producers and consumers with OpenTelemetry links a published event to each receiving service in one trace4, which turns propagation into something you can query and show to an auditor.

A consent you cannot withdraw everywhere is not really consent. Design withdrawal first; recording consent is the easy half.

Partners without event integrations received withdrawals through an authenticated API with mandatory acknowledgement and a retry queue. Unacknowledged withdrawals raised alerts to the partner owner, not just to engineering. Related reading: change data capture and event streams and data contracts.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: start with the data and partner inventory, and make withdrawal the first feature built. Differently: bring partners in earlier. The longest delays came from contract changes and vendors without any way to receive a withdrawal, not from the engineering. FAQ

Common questions

When do the DPDP Rules apply?

The Rules were notified on 13 November 2025 and phase in over 18 months. Consent manager registration applies after one year, and most core obligations for data fiduciaries apply from around May 2027.

What does a consent ledger do?

It records each consent as an immutable event: who consented, to which purpose, under which notice version, when, and through which channel, and records withdrawals the same way. Every system that uses personal data checks it before processing.

How do you prove a withdrawal reached every partner?

Publish withdrawal as an event, require each consumer and partner integration to acknowledge it, and trace the event end to end so the audit trail shows delivery and the action taken.
