Re-architecting a SaaS platform so one tenant can't slow down the rest

A few heavy tenants could degrade the service for everyone, and enterprise buyers were asking for isolation guarantees the platform could not evidence. The goal was tiered tenancy: pooled by default, dedicated where it is worth paying for, with per-tenant limits and isolation proven by tests rather than asserted in a questionnaire.

The situation

A B2B SaaS platform had grown on a single shared architecture: one application deployment, one database cluster, every tenant in the same collections. That served it well while tenants were similar in size. As larger customers arrived, two problems appeared together. A heavy import or reporting job from one tenant could slow the service for everyone. And enterprise prospects began asking, in security reviews, how their data was isolated and whether they could have dedicated infrastructure.

The team's instinct was to give big customers their own copy of everything. That answers the sales question and creates an operations problem: many stacks to upgrade, monitor and pay for.

What had to be true

  • Isolation as a choice per tenant, not one model forced on all.
  • One codebase and one release process across every tier.
  • Per-tenant limits at every shared resource.
  • Tenant context everywhere: in routing, data access, logs, traces and cost.
  • Isolation proven by tests, so the answer to a security review is evidence.

Architecture

AWS's tenant isolation guidance frames the options as silo (dedicated resources), pool (shared resources) and bridge (a mix chosen per layer)1. The design adopted that vocabulary directly: a pooled tier for most tenants and a dedicated tier for those whose requirements justify it, running the same services.

The data flow, end to end

On the data side, MongoDB's guidance compares database-per-tenant with shared collections keyed by a tenant field, and notes that the shared model needs every query to filter by tenant3. That requirement was moved out of individual queries and into one data access layer. Detail in MongoDB multi-tenant SaaS patterns.

The hard part: noisy neighbours at every layer

Noisy neighbours are rarely one bottleneck. Limits had to exist at each shared resource, or load simply moved to the next one. The Kubernetes documentation treats noisy neighbours as a core multi-tenancy concern and points to resource quotas and node isolation as controls2; quotas cap aggregate consumption per namespace5. The layers addressed were:

  • Requests: per-tenant rate and concurrency limits at the gateway.
  • Background work: per-tenant queues or fair scheduling, so one tenant's bulk import cannot starve others.
  • Compute: dedicated namespaces with quotas for the dedicated tier; resource requests and limits in the pool.
  • Database: indexes that start with the tenant key, query timeouts, and heavy reporting moved off the primary path.

None of that is manageable without seeing it per tenant. OpenTelemetry baggage carries key-value context such as a tenant identifier across service boundaries4, which let traces and metrics be broken down by tenant. More in Kubernetes multi-tenancy.

Isolation you cannot test is a claim. Isolation with a failing test when it breaks is a control.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: get tenant context right before anything else, and make isolation a test suite rather than a document. Differently: agree the tier definitions with product and sales earlier. The engineering was straightforward once everyone agreed what "dedicated" promised and who could buy it.

FAQ

Common questions

Should every enterprise customer get a dedicated stack?

No. Dedicated tiers cost more to run and operate. Offer them where isolation, residency or performance requirements justify it, and keep most tenants pooled with strong logical isolation and limits.

How do you prove tenant isolation to a buyer?

With automated tests that attempt cross-tenant access at each layer and fail the build if they succeed, plus the architecture and controls that explain why. Evidence from tests is more convincing than a questionnaire answer.

What causes noisy neighbour problems?

Shared resources without per-tenant limits: database capacity, compute, queues and connection pools. The fix is tenant-aware limits at each shared layer and observability that shows which tenant is consuming what.
