Fixing product search and AI shopping by fixing what the catalogue means

Supplier feeds arrived with their own categories, attribute names and units. Search filters broke, facets contradicted each other and an AI shopping assistant gave confident answers from messy data. The fix was a governed product ontology that every catalogue load is mapped to and validated against.

The situation

An online retailer sold products from many suppliers. Each supplier feed brought its own category tree, attribute names and conventions: one sent "colour", another "color_name", a third put colour in the title. Sizes, materials and units varied by feed. The internal category tree had grown by accretion, with near-duplicate branches and categories that meant different things to different teams.

The symptoms were familiar. Filters returned partial results because half the products lacked the attribute being filtered. Search relied on hand-maintained synonym lists. A new AI shopping assistant made the problem more visible: asked to compare products, it reasoned confidently over data that was inconsistent underneath.

What had to be true

  • One model of meaning for categories and attributes, shared by search, filters, feeds and the assistant.
  • Mapping, not rewriting: supplier data is mapped to the model, with the original preserved.
  • Validation on every load, so bad data is caught before customers see it.
  • Business ownership: merchandisers change the taxonomy through review, without engineering in the loop for every edit.
  • Standards, not a bespoke format, so the model outlives any one tool.

Architecture

The taxonomy was modelled in SKOS, which is designed for concept schemes with broader and narrower relations and preferred and alternative labels1. A deliberately small OWL core covered the classes and properties that needed formal definitions2. The reasoning behind that split is in SKOS vs OWL for enterprise taxonomies.

The data flow, end to end

SHACL describes the conditions data must meet and produces a validation report, which made it a natural release gate for each load3. Patterns for writing shapes are in SHACL validation for knowledge graphs.

The hard part: attributes, not categories

Agreeing a category tree was comparatively quick. Agreeing attributes was not, because attributes are where search filters and assistant answers actually come from. The work that made a difference:

  • Attributes defined per category, with required, recommended and optional tiers, so a shape knows what "complete" means for a sofa versus a shoe.
  • Controlled value lists with preferred and alternative labels, so "navy", "dark blue" and supplier codes resolve to one value while search still matches all of them.
  • Canonical units with conversion at load, never in the search layer.
  • Duplicate and variant handling, the same product from two suppliers, which is an entity resolution problem in its own right; see entity resolution for knowledge graphs.

An AI assistant is only as consistent as the attributes it reads. Many apparent "hallucinations" about products turn out to be faithful readings of contradictory data.

How the engagement ran

Product pages also emitted schema.org Product markup generated from the same model4, so structured data for external search stayed consistent with internal search. More in structured data for AI search.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: start from search logs and the categories that matter commercially, and make validation a gate rather than a report. Differently: put merchandising ownership and the review workflow in place before the model grows, not after. A taxonomy without a clear owner drifts back to where it started.

FAQ

Common questions

Why SKOS rather than OWL for a product taxonomy?

SKOS is built for concept schemes with broader and narrower relations, preferred and alternative labels, which is what categories and synonyms need. OWL is kept for the small set of classes and properties where formal semantics actually matter.

What does SHACL add to a catalogue pipeline?

SHACL expresses the rules a product must meet, such as required attributes per category, allowed values and units, and produces a validation report. That turns data quality into an automated gate on every load.

Does an AI shopping assistant need an ontology?

It needs consistent facts. An ontology gives the assistant normalised attributes and synonyms to filter and compare on, rather than relying on the model to interpret inconsistent supplier text.
