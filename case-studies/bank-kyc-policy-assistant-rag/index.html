Giving KYC analysts cited answers from policy, without giving up control

Onboarding analysts were searching hundreds of policy pages and past case notes for every decision. The goal was an assistant that answers from approved sources only, shows its citations, respects who is allowed to see what, and is measured against questions compliance has signed off.

The situation

A bank's onboarding analysts spent much of each case looking things up. Policy lived across hundreds of pages of procedures, risk appetite statements and local addenda, and the most useful guidance was often in past case notes: how a similar ownership structure or jurisdiction had been handled before. Senior analysts carried that knowledge in their heads; newer analysts asked them, or searched a document portal that matched keywords and returned whole documents.

Customer due diligence obligations come from the FATF Recommendations and their national implementations, which require institutions to identify and verify customers and beneficial owners1. None of that changes because an assistant exists. The aim was narrower and more useful: help analysts find the right rule and the right precedent faster, with the evidence in front of them.

What had to be true

  • Answers come from approved sources only, and every answer cites them.
  • Entitlements are enforced at retrieval: a user only ever sees passages they could already open in the source system.
  • The analyst decides. The assistant informs a decision; it never records one.
  • Quality is measured before release, against questions compliance agrees are representative.
  • Everything is auditable: question, retrieved sources, model version and answer.

Architecture

The pattern is retrieval-augmented generation: a retriever selects relevant passages and a generator answers conditioned on them, which keeps knowledge in an index that can be updated without retraining2. The work was in making each layer governable.

The data flow, end to end

Hybrid retrieval mattered because KYC questions mix exact terms (a form name, a jurisdiction, a document type) with paraphrase. Keyword search finds the former and vectors the latter. The detail is in hybrid search for RAG and choosing embedding models.

The hard part: permissions that survive retrieval

The tempting shortcut is one index for everyone and an instruction to the model not to reveal restricted material. That is not access control. OWASP's Top 10 for LLM applications lists sensitive information disclosure and vector and embedding weaknesses as distinct risks for exactly this kind of system3. The approach was:

  • Carry the source system's permissions onto every chunk at index time, and re-sync them when they change.
  • Apply the entitlement filter inside the retrieval query, so restricted passages are never ranked, let alone sent to the model.
  • Treat case notes as more sensitive than policy by default, with access scoped by business line and region.
  • Test it: evaluation cases that ask, as a restricted user, for content they should not see, and fail the release if anything leaks.

The same thinking applies to retrieved text as untrusted input; see prompt injection defence.

If a user could not open the document, the model should never read it on their behalf. Enforce that in the query, not the prompt.

How it ran

Framing the work against the NIST AI RMF's govern, map, measure and manage functions gave risk and compliance teams a familiar structure for their review4. The release checklist followed RAG to production, and the evaluation design followed evaluation sets that predict production.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: write the evaluation set before the first prompt, and put entitlements in the data path from day one. Differently: start the source clean-up sooner. Superseded policy versions and inconsistent case notes caused more wrong answers than the model did, and fixing content is slower than fixing code.

FAQ

Common questions

Can a RAG assistant make KYC decisions?

It should not. In this design the assistant retrieves and cites policy and precedent; the analyst makes and records the decision, and the assistant's output is evidence in the file, not the decision itself.

How do you stop the assistant showing documents a user is not allowed to see?

Filter at retrieval time using the same entitlements as the source systems, before anything reaches the model. Prompt instructions alone are not an access control.

How do you know the assistant is good enough to release?

Build an evaluation set of real analyst questions with expected sources and answers, have compliance sign it off, and require every change to pass it before release.
