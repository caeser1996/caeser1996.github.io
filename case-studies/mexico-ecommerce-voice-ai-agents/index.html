Answering in under a breath: voice AI agents for a Mexican e-commerce marketplace

A Mexican marketplace took most of its customer calls about one thing: where is my order. Wait times grew every sales season. Voice AI agents could answer those calls, but only if they sounded natural in Mexican Spanish, responded fast enough to feel like a conversation, handed over to people gracefully and respected callers' data rights. The architecture had to make all four true at once.

The situation

A marketplace in Mexico sold electronics, home goods and fashion from thousands of sellers, with deliveries handled by several carriers. Its contact centre took calls in Spanish, and most of them were about the same few things: where an order was, when it would arrive, how to change the address, how to start a return. During sales seasons, queues grew long enough that callers hung up and tried again, which made the queues longer.

Why now. On 29 October 2026, Deepgram announced it is acquiring Daily, the company behind Pipecat, a widely used open-source framework for real-time voice agents1. Both companies said Pipecat will stay under its BSD licence and move to an independent foundation whose steering committee includes Deepgram's competitors2. For a team choosing a voice agent stack, that settled the main worry about building on an open framework. On the legal side, Mexico's new personal data protection law took effect in March 202534, and legal commentary notes a right to object to automated processing that significantly affects people without human involvement3. Callers would always need a route to a person.

What had to be true

  • Natural Mexican Spanish, including regional accents, background noise and code-switching with English product names.
  • Conversational speed: replies quick enough that silence never feels like a dropped call.
  • Grounded answers only: order status from the systems, never from the model's imagination.
  • A person on request, with the conversation so far passed across.
  • Clear data rules for recordings and transcripts, and no lock-in to one speech vendor.

The data flow, end to end

The hard part: latency you can feel

In text chat, a two-second delay is fine. On a phone call it feels like the line has dropped. The delay a caller hears is the sum of several stages: detecting that they stopped speaking, finishing speech recognition, the model's first tokens, any tool calls, and the first audio from speech synthesis. Each stage looked acceptable alone; together they were too slow.

The team set a latency budget per stage and traced every call end to end, so each turn showed exactly where the time went, in the same way distributed tracing shows which service in a request is slow5. That made the fixes obvious: stream everything instead of waiting for whole sentences; prefetch the caller's recent orders as soon as they are verified, so the first tool call is usually instant; use a smaller, faster model for routine turns; and play a short natural acknowledgement while a slow carrier API responds.

Turn-taking was the other half. Callers interrupt, pause mid-sentence and talk over the agent. The framework's interruption handling let the agent stop speaking when the caller started, and tuning silence detection for Spanish speech patterns cut the most awkward moments. See model routing and prompt caching for latency.

Voice agents are judged in hundreds of milliseconds. Budget latency per stage, trace every turn, and stream everything that can be streamed.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: build the evaluation set from real call recordings before choosing providers. Speech models that tested well on clean audio struggled with real calls. Differently: trace latency per stage from the first prototype, not after the first complaints. Related: evaluating AI agents, an agent-ready travel marketplace, today's tech news and where I work.

FAQ

Common questions

What is Pipecat?

An open-source framework for building real-time voice and multimodal AI agents, made by Daily. In October 2026 Deepgram announced it is acquiring Daily, and both companies say Pipecat stays under its BSD licence with governance moving to an independent foundation.

How fast does a voice agent need to respond?

Fast enough that the pause feels like a person thinking, not a system failing. That means budgeting latency across speech recognition, the model, tools and speech synthesis, and streaming each stage rather than waiting for the previous one to finish.

Does Mexican data protection law affect voice AI?

Yes. Mexico's new personal data protection law took effect in March 2025. Legal commentary notes a right to object to automated processing that significantly affects people without human involvement, so callers should be able to reach a person, and recordings need a clear purpose and retention period.
