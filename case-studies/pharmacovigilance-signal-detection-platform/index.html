From spreadsheets to an auditable pharmacovigilance signal platform

A drug safety team tracked potential signals across spreadsheets, email and slide decks. Each evaluation meant a bespoke data request and weeks of analysis. The goal was one platform that detects candidate signals, keeps the reasoning auditable and lets the team test them quickly against real-world data.

The situation

A pharmaceutical company's drug safety team handled case processing well; their safety database and regulatory reporting were mature. Signal management was not. Potential signals arrived from periodic report reviews, literature, partners and occasional regulator queries, and were tracked in a spreadsheet. Evaluating one meant a custom data request, an analyst working for weeks, and conclusions summarised in slides that did not record which data or method they came from.

The head of safety wanted three things: consistent screening, faster evaluation and a record any inspector could follow from first observation to final decision.

What had to be true

  • Every signal has one record, from source to decision, with who did what and why.
  • Screening is consistent: the same methods on the same schedule, not ad hoc queries.
  • Detection is not confused with evidence. Report counts start investigations; they don't conclude them.
  • Evaluation is fast and reproducible, using standardised real-world data and versioned study code.
  • Validated where it matters, with the computerised system controls a regulated safety function expects.

The EU's good pharmacovigilance practice guidance on signal management frames the lifecycle the platform needed to support, and EMA presentations on it emphasise that marketing authorisation holders should keep an audit trail of their signal management activities1.

The hard part: not over-reading spontaneous reports

Spontaneous reports are the backbone of signal detection and also easy to misuse. The FDA states plainly that a report does not mean the product caused the event, that report counts cannot establish how often an event occurs, and that reports can be duplicated, incomplete or stimulated by publicity2. The platform's design reflected that:

  • Disproportionality scores were labelled as screening statistics, never as risk estimates, everywhere they appeared.
  • Every flag required case-level review by a safety physician before becoming a validated signal.
  • Evaluation used designed studies on population data, not more report counting.

For evaluation, self-controlled designs suited many acute safety questions because they compare time periods within the same people, removing confounding by stable characteristics3. Negative control outcomes and empirical calibration showed whether residual bias could explain a result4. The designs are compared in real-world evidence study designs.

A signal platform's most important feature is the line it draws between "worth investigating" and "evidence of harm". Blur that line and you get both false alarms and false reassurance.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: fix the record-keeping first, then add analytics. Differently: agree data access with real-world data partners at the start. Contracts and governance for external data take longer than building the screening pipeline, and evaluations wait on them. The data side is described in federated health analytics and OMOP data quality checks.

FAQ

Common questions

What is a safety signal?

Information suggesting a new potentially causal association, or a new aspect of a known association, between a medicine and an event, that warrants further investigation. Signal management covers detecting, validating, analysing, prioritising and assessing those signals.

Can spontaneous reports show how often an adverse event happens?

No. The FDA notes that counts of reports in FAERS do not establish how often an event occurs and that a report does not mean the product caused the event. Spontaneous reports are for detection; incidence and causality need other data and designs.

How does real-world data help evaluate a signal?

Standardised data such as OMOP lets teams run designed studies, for example self-controlled designs for acute events, with diagnostics and negative controls, to see whether a signal holds up in a population.
