Getting an ambient AI scribe through security review and into the EHR

Clinicians were finishing notes in the evening, and every AI scribe pilot had stalled in security review. The goal was a private ambient documentation pipeline that drafted notes for clinician sign-off, ran on infrastructure the organisation controlled, and could prove what happened to every recording.

The situation

A multi-specialty provider group had a documentation problem everyone could describe: clinicians finishing notes after clinic, and patients getting less eye contact than they deserved. Ambient AI scribes looked like the answer, and several vendor pilots had been started. Each one stalled in the same place: security and privacy review. Reviewers could not get clear answers about where audio went, who could see transcripts, how long anything was kept, or how a wrong sentence in a note would be caught.

The ask was not "pick a better vendor". It was to design a pipeline the organisation could own end to end, so those questions had answers before the first recording.

What had to be true

  • Consent first: no recording without a recorded patient consent, and an easy way to stop.
  • Data stays inside the boundary: audio, transcripts and drafts processed on infrastructure the organisation controls.
  • A human signs every note: the model drafts, the clinician decides.
  • Least privilege into the EHR: the app sees the current patient and encounter, nothing more.
  • Provable history: who recorded, what was generated, what was changed, what was filed.

Architecture

The design split the problem into capture, private inference, review and filing, with audit running across all of them.

Speech recognition used a Whisper-class model, a family trained on large-scale weakly supervised audio and released with open weights1, which made private hosting practical. Note drafting ran on vLLM, whose PagedAttention memory management is designed for high-throughput serving on limited GPU memory2. Serving patterns are in LLM inference serving with vLLM and private LLMs on-premises.

The data flow, end to end

The app launched inside the EHR using SMART on FHIR, requesting only the patient and encounter context and the narrow scopes it needed3. The signed note was filed as a FHIR DocumentReference4. More on scope design in SMART on FHIR authorisation for AI apps.

The hard part: trust in the draft, not the transcript

Transcription quality was rarely the argument. The concern was a fluent note that said something the patient never said. Three choices addressed it:

  • Grounded drafting: every sentence in the draft carries a link to the transcript span it came from, and sentences without one are highlighted.
  • Clinician-scored evaluation: a curated set of consented encounters, scored by clinicians for omissions and unsupported statements, gated every model and prompt change. See evaluation sets that predict production.
  • Edit capture: what clinicians changed was recorded, so recurring corrections fed back into templates and the evaluation set.

An AI scribe is approved on the strength of its failure handling. Reviewers need to see how a wrong sentence is caught, not how rarely it appears in a demo.

How it ran

Audit records followed the shape of the FHIR AuditEvent resource (who, what, when, outcome), so the trail could be queried alongside other EHR audit data5.

What I'd do again, and differently

Again: take the design to the security board before building anything, and treat evaluation as infrastructure. Differently: involve clinicians in template design earlier. Most edits were about structure and specialty conventions, not model accuracy, and that work is cheaper before the pilot than during it. FAQ

Common questions

Why run an ambient scribe on private infrastructure?

It keeps audio and transcripts inside a boundary the organisation already governs, which shortens security and privacy review and avoids sending identifiable conversations to a third party by default.

Does the AI write directly into the medical record?

No. The model drafts a note with links back to the transcript, and a clinician reviews, edits and signs it. Only the approved note is written to the EHR.

How do you know the drafts are good enough?

A curated evaluation set of consented encounters, scored by clinicians, is run before every model or prompt change. Changes that regress on omissions or unsupported statements do not ship.
