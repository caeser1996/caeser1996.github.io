Case study · HR and payroll software · Singapore and Malaysia

Two platforms, one product: integrating an acquired HR and payroll SaaS in Southeast Asia

A Singapore-based HR and payroll software company bought a smaller Malaysian competitor for its customers and local payroll expertise. The deal assumed the two platforms would merge within a year. They had different clouds, identity systems, tenancy models and payroll engines, and both held sensitive employee data under different national laws. The goal was to integrate without losing customers, breaking payroll runs or moving data unlawfully.

Case study · AI software · Brazil

Not tied to one chip: accelerator-portable inference for a Brazilian AI software company

A Brazilian software company served its own fine-tuned language models to retail and banking customers from GPUs in a São Paulo cloud region. Its stack assumed one GPU family and one provider. New US licensing for advanced chips shipped to Brazil, and growing interest in cloud providers' own accelerators, made that assumption a business risk. The goal was inference that could move between accelerators and providers without a rewrite, and without data leaving Brazil.

Case study · Healthcare and research · Japan

One front door for research: securing lab-deployed AI tools at a Japanese university hospital

A Japanese university hospital ran research AI tools that individual labs had installed themselves: imaging annotation servers, notebook servers, model demos. Some were reachable from the internet, outside the hospital's security operations. When AI agents were reported to have found and exploited a zero-day in a medical imaging tool on exactly that kind of server, the goal became clear: find every research service, put it behind one front door, and do it without stopping the research.

Case study · Consumer credit · European Union

Fast, lawful and reviewable: creditworthiness assessment at checkout for an EU buy now, pay later provider

A buy now, pay later provider operating in several EU countries approved purchases at checkout with a light-touch risk check. The revised Consumer Credit Directive brings that kind of credit into scope from 20 November 2026, with a proper creditworthiness assessment, limits on the data that may be used and a right to human review of automated decisions. The goal was to meet all three without making checkout slow.

Analysis · Analysis · Regulation and AI architecture

The FCA's line on AI agents: what it means for the architecture of agents that move money

On 24 November the UK Financial Conduct Authority set out its approach to agentic AI. There are no new rules, but the message is sharp: an agent that initiates payments, arranges credit or gives advice may need authorisation, calling yourself a technology provider changes nothing, and regulated firms stay accountable for agents they build or buy. That is a regulatory statement, but most of what it asks for is architecture.
