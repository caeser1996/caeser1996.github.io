Case study · Insurance · Singapore

When the suite goes down: keeping a Singapore digital insurer running through a productivity SaaS outage

A Singapore digital insurer ran its customer-facing platforms on its own cloud estate, with careful failover. Everything around them, email, chat, documents, identity and increasingly AI assistants, ran on one productivity suite. The insurer had planned for its own systems failing, not for that suite failing. A long outage at a major provider made the gap obvious, and Singapore's business continuity expectations for financial institutions made it urgent.

Case study · Financial services · South Korea

Inference is a server too: securing self-hosted LLM serving for a South Korean fintech

A South Korean fintech moved its AI features from external APIs to open-weight models served on its own GPUs, to keep customer data in-house and control cost. The data science team had made it work. The security team had not yet looked. When inference engines like vLLM and SGLang started appearing in advisories and breach reports, the company treated its model servers as what they are: internet-adjacent services running complex code, with customer data passing through them.

Case study · Retail and consumer credit · Chile

Ready for the new rules: preparing a Chilean omnichannel retailer for personal data protection law 21.719

A Chilean retailer sold in stores and online, ran a loyalty programme and offered its own store credit card. Personal data sat in dozens of systems built over two decades under an older, lighter law. With Ley 21.719 creating a new data protection agency and stronger rights from December 2026, the company needed to know what data it held and why, answer customers' requests reliably, and handle a breach quickly, without stopping the business.

Case study · Financial services · Japan

Every door has a lock and an owner: governing third-party access for a Japanese online securities firm

A Japanese online securities firm ran on AWS and relied on a long list of suppliers: a data platform, an observability vendor, a managed service provider for office devices, SaaS tools connected to its email and files. Each had been given access when it was onboarded, and nobody had a single view of who could reach what. When attackers used one data platform's trusted access to raid its customers' AWS accounts, the firm set out to know, narrow, watch and be able to cut every supplier's access.
