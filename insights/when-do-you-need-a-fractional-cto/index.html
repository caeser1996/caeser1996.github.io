When do you need a fractional CTO? (And when you don't)

A fractional CTO is a good answer to a specific set of problems, and a poor answer to others. Here is how I tell the difference, and what the work looks like once it starts.

Most founders and executives I speak with do not start by asking for a fractional CTO. They start with a symptom. Releases keep slipping. The AI pilot looks good in a demo and falls apart on real data. An investor asks a question about the architecture and nobody in the room can answer it with confidence. Somewhere in that conversation the question comes up: do we need a CTO, and can we afford one?

This piece is my honest answer. I work as a fractional CTO and principal engineer, so I have an obvious interest, but I turn down work that is a bad fit, because a bad fit damages both sides. Here is how I decide.

What a fractional CTO actually does

A fractional CTO provides senior technology leadership on a part-time basis. The scope is the same as a full-time CTO: technical strategy, architecture, hiring, engineering process, security posture, and being the person who can explain technology decisions to a board or an investor. The difference is the time commitment and the length of the arrangement.

In practice, the job splits into three kinds of work:

  • Decisions that are expensive to reverse. Choice of cloud, data model, build versus buy, the shape of the first platform. These deserve senior judgement even when the team is small.
  • Building the team that will own the system. Hiring the first senior engineers, setting up review and release practices, and mentoring people into leadership.
  • Translation. Turning business goals into a technical roadmap, and technical risk into language the board can act on.

What a fractional CTO is not: a cheaper developer, or a consultant who writes a report and disappears. If the engagement has no ownership of outcomes, it is advisory, which is useful but different.

Signs you probably need one

These are the patterns I see most often in companies where a fractional CTO pays for itself quickly.

  1. Technical bets are being made without anyone senior in the room. A founder with a commercial background is choosing between vendors, frameworks or agencies based on sales pitches.
  2. The team is good but junior. Strong engineers who have never designed a system for scale, regulated data or a multi-year roadmap.
  3. An AI initiative has stalled between pilot and production. The demo worked, but nobody can say how good the answers are, what they cost or whether they respect data permissions. I wrote more about this in From RAG demo to production: a checklist.
  4. The platform is struggling to grow. Latency spikes at peak, incidents are frequent and every release feels risky. I have designed and scaled systems handling more than a million transactions a day, and the fixes are usually architectural, not heroic.
  5. A fundraise or acquisition is coming. Investors will run technical due diligence, and it is far better to find the problems yourself first.
  6. The previous CTO has left. Someone needs to hold the technical agenda while you decide what the permanent role should look like.

And when you don't

Just as often, a fractional CTO is the wrong answer. I would steer you elsewhere if:

  • You need someone in technical decisions every day. If architecture questions come up hourly and the team is blocked without an answer, you need a full-time leader.
  • The problem is pure delivery capacity. If the plan is clear and you simply need more hands, hire engineers or a good delivery partner.
  • The CTO is central to the company story. For a deep-tech company where the CTO is a co-founder in the eyes of investors, a part-time role sends the wrong signal.
  • Nobody will act on the advice. If leadership is not ready to change how decisions are made, the engagement will produce documents, not outcomes.

A useful test: write down the three technology decisions you will make in the next six months. If you cannot name them, start with a short assessment rather than an ongoing role.

Fractional, interim, full-time or agency?

These options are often confused. They solve different problems.

OptionBest forWatch out for
Fractional CTOSenior judgement on strategy, architecture and hiring, part-time over monthsNeeds a team that can execute between sessions
Interim CTOFull-time cover for a defined period, often after a departure or during a crisisHigher cost; risk of the role becoming permanent by accident
Full-time CTOCompanies where technology leadership is needed daily and is central to the businessLong and expensive hiring process; hard to reverse a wrong hire
Agency or dev shopDelivery capacity against a clear planIncentivised to build more, not to question whether it should be built
AdvisorA sounding board for the founder or existing CTONo ownership of execution

Many companies use a combination. A common pattern is a fractional CTO who sets the architecture and hires the first senior engineers, with an agency adding capacity for a defined build.

What the first 90 days look like

Every engagement is different, but the shape is usually similar. I work in three phases.

Days 1 to 30: understand

  • Interviews with the founders, the leadership team and every engineer.
  • An audit of the code, infrastructure, data and security posture. I read the code; I do not rely on slides.
  • A short written assessment: what is working, what is risky, and the three to five decisions that matter most.

Days 31 to 60: decide

  • A technical roadmap tied to business milestones, not a wish list of rewrites.
  • Architecture decision records for the big choices, so the reasoning survives after I leave.
  • A hiring plan, and the first job descriptions and interview loops.

Days 61 to 90: deliver the first slice

  • One thin, production-grade piece of the roadmap shipped, so the team sees the new approach working.
  • Basic engineering hygiene in place: CI/CD, observability, on-call, review practices.
  • A regular rhythm with the leadership team and, where relevant, a board update.

The first slice matters more than the documents. A roadmap that has not touched production is a hypothesis.

How to think about cost

I will not quote numbers here, because the right model depends on the stage of the company and the scope. The more useful question is how to structure the arrangement so that you pay for outcomes.

  • Retainer by days per month. The most common model. Predictable for both sides, and easy to scale up for a fundraise or down once the team is stable.
  • Fixed-scope assessment. A two to four week review with a defined deliverable. A good way to start if you are unsure.
  • Project-based build. When the main need is a specific platform or AI system designed and delivered, then handed over.

Compare the cost against the real alternative: the time and money of a full-time executive search, the cost of a wrong hire, or the cost of rebuilding a system that was designed badly in year one.

Handing over cleanly

A fractional engagement should have an end in sight. The goal is a team that runs the system confidently without me. That usually means one of three outcomes:

  1. A full-time CTO is hired, often with my help on the search and interviews, and I hand over the roadmap, decision records and relationships.
  2. An internal leader is promoted into the role, after months of mentoring and shared ownership.
  3. The engagement steps down to advisory, a few hours a month as a sounding board.

The handover artefacts matter: architecture decision records, runbooks, an up-to-date system diagram, a security and risk register, and a hiring plan. If those exist, the next leader starts in week one rather than month three.

Questions to ask any fractional CTO

  • Will you read our code, or only talk to the team?
  • What will we have in writing after 30 days?
  • Which decisions would you expect to make in the first quarter?
  • How do you hand over, and what does success look like when you leave?
  • Where would you tell us we do not need you?

The last question is the most revealing. Anyone who cannot answer it is selling time, not judgement.

What a good engagement feels like

From the client side, a few things tell you the arrangement is working. Decisions that used to drag on for weeks get made, with the reasoning written down. The engineering team knows what matters this quarter and why. Board and investor conversations about technology become shorter and calmer. And the team needs you a little less each month, which is exactly the point.

If, after a few months, everything still routes through the fractional CTO, something is wrong. Either the team has not been given ownership, or the role has quietly become a full-time job that should be filled by a full-time hire.

FAQ

Common questions

What is the difference between a fractional CTO and an interim CTO?

A fractional CTO works part-time over a longer period and usually sits alongside the existing team. An interim CTO is typically full-time for a short, defined period, often covering a departure or a crisis.

How many days a week does a fractional CTO work?

It varies with the stage of the company. Many engagements start at one to two days a week, with more time during the first month while the assessment and roadmap are being built.

When should a startup hire a full-time CTO instead?

When technology decisions are needed daily, the engineering team is large enough to need constant leadership, or the CTO role is core to fundraising and the company story. A good fractional CTO will tell you when that point has arrived.

Can a fractional CTO help with technical due diligence?

Yes. Due diligence is one of the most common fractional engagements, either preparing a company for investor scrutiny or assessing a target on behalf of an investor.
