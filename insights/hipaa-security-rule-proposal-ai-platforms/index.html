The proposed HIPAA Security Rule overhaul: what healthcare AI teams should build now

The biggest proposed change to the HIPAA Security Rule in years is still not final. That is not a reason to wait. Most of what it asks for is what a well-run healthcare AI platform should already have.

In January 2025 HHS published a proposed overhaul of the HIPAA Security Rule, the first major update in many years1. Healthcare organisations have been watching it since. As of this writing it still isn't final, and commentators report the expected timing has slipped, with regulatory agenda targets moving into 202734.

My view as an architect working on health data: stop waiting for the final text. Most of the proposal describes controls a healthcare AI platform should have anyway. Confirm the legal detail with counsel; build the engineering now.

What the proposal would change

Based on HHS's own summary and the proposed rule, the headline changes include12:

AreaProposed changeWhat it means for an AI platform
EncryptionRequired for ePHI at rest and in transit, with limited exceptions, instead of "addressable"Every store holding ePHI is encrypted, including vector indexes, caches and logs
Multi-factor authenticationRequired for access to systems with ePHI, with limited exceptionsMFA on consoles, notebooks, data tools and admin APIs, not just the main app
Asset inventory and network mapWritten, maintained inventory of technology assets and data flowsYou must know every place ePHI travels, including model providers
Testing and recoveryRegular vulnerability scanning, penetration testing and restoration expectationsRecovery is tested, not assumed

Treat the specifics as proposed. The final rule could change, be scaled back or be withdrawn4.

The AI-specific blind spots

Traditional HIPAA programmes were built around EHRs and databases. AI systems add places for ePHI to hide that inventories often miss:

  • Embeddings and vector indexes built from clinical notes. They are derived from ePHI and should be protected like it.
  • Prompt and response logs, which can contain full patient details if content capture is switched on. My LLM observability post covers capturing metadata without content.
  • Evaluation datasets copied from production to test quality, often into less controlled environments.
  • External model providers, which need appropriate agreements and a clear record of what data reaches them.
  • Agent tool calls that move data between systems, which should be scoped and logged as in securing MCP servers.

If your asset inventory doesn't list your vector database, your prompt logs and your model providers, it is incomplete, whatever the final rule says.

What I'd build now

  1. A data-flow map for every AI use case: where ePHI enters, every store it lands in, and every service it reaches.
  2. Encryption everywhere by default, enforced in infrastructure as code so new stores can't be created unencrypted.
  3. MFA through a single identity provider for every tool that touches ePHI, including data science environments.
  4. An AI gateway that redacts or blocks ePHI before it reaches providers without the right agreements, and logs every call.
  5. Synthetic or de-identified data for evaluation wherever possible, with production samples handled under the same controls as production.
  6. Tested restores of the data platform and vector indexes on a schedule, with the results recorded.

These sit naturally on top of a governed clinical data platform like the one in FHIR to OMOP.

The cost of waiting

Commentary suggests organisations may get a limited window to comply once a final rule is published3. Retrofitting encryption, MFA and an accurate inventory across a sprawling AI estate in a few months is far harder than building them in as the estate grows. And the risk these controls reduce exists today, rule or no rule.

FAQ

Common questions

Is the new HIPAA Security Rule final?

Not as of this writing. HHS published the proposed rule in January 2025 and the comment period closed in March 2025. Commentators report the final action has slipped, with regulatory agenda targets moving into 2027. Check the Federal Register for the current status.

What would the proposed rule change?

Among other things, it would make encryption of ePHI and multi-factor authentication required rather than addressable in most cases, require a written technology asset inventory and network map, and add expectations around testing, vulnerability scanning and recovery.

Should healthcare AI teams act before it is final?

Yes, for the engineering basics. Encryption, MFA, an accurate inventory of where ePHI flows, and tested recovery reduce risk now and would ease compliance if the rule is finalised in a similar form.
