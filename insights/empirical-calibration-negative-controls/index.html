Empirical calibration explained: why observational health studies need negative controls

Real-world evidence is moving from research papers into regulatory submissions, payer decisions and product claims. Yet most observational studies report confidence intervals as if their design had no systematic error. OHDSI's work on method validity shows how badly that assumption fails, and offers a practical fix: test the design on questions with known answers, then calibrate. Here is how it works, and what it means for the teams building evidence platforms.

The problem: honest-looking intervals that are not

A 95% confidence interval from an observational study accounts for random error: the chance variation from studying a sample. It does not account for systematic error from confounding, selection or measurement problems in the data and design. In observational healthcare data those systematic errors are rarely zero, so the stated 95% interval often contains the true effect far less than 95% of the time.

The Book of OHDSI makes the point with evidence rather than argument. OHDSI ran the methods in its Methods Library, with various analysis choices, against a benchmark of control questions on four large databases. Several methods separated positive from negative controls well, but most showed high type 1 error and confidence interval coverage well below 95% in most settings1.

The book's conclusion is blunt: without empirical evaluation, which is true for almost all published observational studies, we should assume it is likely that the true effect is not inside the reported 95% interval1.

The fix: ask questions you already know the answer to

The idea, set out by Schuemie and colleagues, is to run the same study design on control questions whose answer is known, and measure how wrong the design is2.

  • Negative controls are exposure and outcome pairs believed to have no causal relationship. A perfect design would centre their estimates on a relative risk of one. The spread and shift of the actual estimates is the design's systematic error in this data.
  • Positive controls have a known effect. Because real ones are scarce, OHDSI synthesises them by injecting additional outcomes into negative controls, so the true effect size is known1.

Empirical calibration then fits a model of that systematic error and adjusts the p-values and confidence intervals of the real outcomes accordingly3. In OHDSI's evaluation, calibration restored type 1 error and coverage to close to their nominal values, at the cost of some extra type 2 error and less precision1.

How it fits into a study pipeline

OHDSI provides the pieces as open-source R packages: EmpiricalCalibration fits the systematic error distribution and calibrates estimates4, and MethodEvaluation synthesises positive controls and computes performance metrics5. Because they work on the OMOP Common Data Model, the same study package can run across data partners and calibrate separately in each database.

What it means for the people building evidence platforms

For engineering and data leaders, the implications are practical rather than statistical.

Platform decisionWhy calibration changes it
Control lists as versioned assetsNegative controls need clinical review and reuse; treat them like code, with owners and history
Blinding built into the workflowResults for outcomes of interest stay hidden until diagnostics pass
Compute budgeted for controlsRunning dozens of controls per outcome multiplies the work; plan capacity for it
Report both calibrated and uncalibratedReviewers can see how much systematic error the design carried
Calibrate per databaseSystematic error differs by data source; one calibration does not fit all

Wide calibrated intervals are a feature. They tell you, before anyone acts on a result, that this design and this data cannot answer the question well.

Regulators have set out how they think about real-world evidence; the FDA's framework, for example, focuses on whether real-world data are fit for use and whether study designs can provide adequate evidence6. Measured systematic error is one of the strongest answers to the second question an evidence team can give.

The CTO's take

If your organisation produces or buys real-world evidence, ask one question of every study: what happened on the negative controls? If there is no answer, the confidence intervals are optimistic by an unknown amount. Building controls, blinding and calibration into the platform costs compute and some process, and it is the cheapest credibility you can buy. FAQ

Common questions

What is a negative control?

An exposure and outcome pair believed to have no causal relationship. If a study design applied to it finds an effect, that apparent effect measures the design's systematic error in that data.

What does empirical calibration do?

It fits the distribution of estimates for negative controls, and positive controls where used, and adjusts the p-values and confidence intervals of the outcomes of interest so they account for the systematic error observed, not only random error.

Does calibration make a bad study good?

No. It makes the uncertainty honest. A design with large systematic error will produce wide calibrated intervals, which is the correct signal that the question cannot be answered well with that design and data.
