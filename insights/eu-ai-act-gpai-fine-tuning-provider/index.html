Fine-tuning a foundation model? When the EU AI Act makes you a general-purpose AI provider

Most companies building on foundation models assume the EU's general-purpose AI rules are for the labs. Usually they are. But the Commission's guidelines draw a line where heavy modification turns a downstream builder into a provider, and since August 2026 the AI Office can enforce those rules.

Picture a scale-up that takes an open-weight model, continues pre-training it on a large domain corpus, fine-tunes it further and offers it to customers across Europe. Is it a deployer of someone else's model, or the provider of a new general-purpose AI model? Under the EU AI Act the answer changes the paperwork considerably, and it now carries enforcement risk.

This is an architect's reading of public guidance and law firm commentary, not legal advice.

Where things stand

The AI Act's obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models have applied since 2 August 2025. The Commission said the AI Office was preparing its compliance assessments ahead of enforcement of those rules from August 20262. A Cloud Security Alliance research note published on 3 August 2026 summarised the AI Office's powers as including requests for documentation, model evaluations, corrective measures and fines7.

Alongside the law sit two practical instruments. The GPAI Code of Practice, published on 10 July 2025, has three chapters: transparency, copyright, and safety and security. The first two give all GPAI providers a way to demonstrate compliance with Article 53; the third applies only to providers of models with systemic risk under Article 5516. The transparency chapter includes a Model Documentation Form1. The Commission also finalised a template for the public summary of training data5.

The line between deployer and provider

The Commission's guidelines on GPAI providers address downstream modification directly. As summarised by commentators34:

  • An indicative criterion is whether the training compute used for the modification exceeds one third of the compute used to train the original model.
  • If the original compute isn't known, one third of the threshold for presuming a model is general-purpose is used instead.
  • Minor changes typically don't make you a provider.
  • If you do become a provider, your obligations generally concern the modification: documentation, training data summary and copyright policy covering the additional compute and data. Modifying a model with systemic risk brings the full systemic-risk obligations.
What you doLikely position
Prompting, RAG, tool use, agents on a hosted modelNot modifying the model
Light fine-tuning or adapters on modest dataTypically a minor change
Large continued pre-training or extensive fine-tuningCheck the compute ratio carefully
Modifying a model already classed as systemic-riskGet specific legal advice early

Most teams are nowhere near the line. The ones that are usually know it, because they are spending serious money on GPUs. That spend should come with a compliance budget.

Why this matters even if you are well below it

First, customers ask. In enterprise procurement, "we use RAG, not fine-tuning" is a much easier answer with evidence behind it. Second, upstream providers' documentation flows down to you: the Model Documentation Form exists partly so downstream providers can understand the models they build on1. Third, the same records support obligations that apply to your AI systems regardless of the model rules, such as the literacy duty in Article 4 and the timeline in the Digital Omnibus roadmap.

What I'd put in place

  1. Log training compute for every model you train or tune, with the method used to estimate it. It's cheap to capture at the time and very hard to reconstruct later.
  2. Keep a model lineage record: base model and version, upstream documentation received, datasets, licences and changes. The supply chain practices in AI model supply chain security overlap heavily.
  3. Document data provenance and copyright handling for any data you train on, including how rights reservations are respected.
  4. Prefer adaptation over retraining where it meets the need. RAG and lighter tuning are usually cheaper as well as simpler to govern; see long context vs RAG.
  5. Map once, reuse often. Tie these records into a framework such as ISO 42001 so the same evidence serves EU, US and Gulf requirements.

FAQ

Common questions

When does fine-tuning make you a GPAI provider under the EU AI Act?

According to summaries of the Commission's guidelines, an indicative criterion is that the training compute used for the modification exceeds one third of the compute used to train the original model. Where that figure is unknown, one third of the threshold for presuming a model is general-purpose is used instead. Minor changes typically don't reclassify a downstream modifier.

What does a downstream modifier have to do if it becomes a provider?

Summaries of the guidelines say the obligations relate to the modification: documentation, a training data summary and a copyright policy covering the additional compute and data. A modifier of a model with systemic risk faces the full systemic-risk obligations.

Is the GPAI Code of Practice mandatory?

No. It is a voluntary tool published in July 2025, with chapters on transparency, copyright, and safety and security, that providers can use to demonstrate compliance with Articles 53 and 55. The Commission and the AI Board have confirmed it as an adequate voluntary tool.
