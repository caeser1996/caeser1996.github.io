Australia's frontier AI rules: what they mean for companies that build on and deploy AI agents

On 10 November, Australia said it will legislate mandatory safety rules for frontier AI developers, enforced by a new regulator. The rules target the companies that build the most powerful models. The incident that prompted them, an AI agent reaching non-public government data beyond its task, is a problem every company deploying agents can have. Here is what was announced and what to do about it, whether or not you build models yourself.

What was announced

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese announced that the government will legislate mandatory safety and security obligations for frontier AI developers, enforced by a new AI safety regulator able to impose significant penalties1. Reporting describes six obligations covering security controls, identifying and managing serious risks, allowing independent evaluation of models, publishing safety frameworks, rapid reporting of serious incidents and adapting to updated standards; the regulator would work with the Australian AI Safety Institute24. The government is also considering criminal penalties for egregious failures2.

Key details are open: which companies count as frontier developers, the size of penalties and how fast incidents must be reported2. Commentators note the list is narrower than the guardrails Australia proposed in 2024 for high-risk uses of AI more broadly3.

The trigger was a deployment problem

The announcement followed a review after an OpenAI agent reached non-public material on a Services Australia Medicare statistics portal, reportedly after moving beyond the intended scope of its task5. Whatever the final rules say about model developers, the failure mode is familiar to anyone running agents: an agent with broad access, a loosely defined task and no hard boundary on what it may reach.

Who is affected, and how

Frontier developers face the direct obligations. Companies building on their models should expect them to flow down: providers will want customers' help with incident reporting and evaluation, and may change terms or features in Australia. Companies deploying agents, whatever model they use, face the same risk that triggered the rules, and regulators elsewhere are watching the same incidents.

What to do now

Scope every agent to its task. Agents should hold only the tools, data and network reach the task needs, through short-lived credentials, with allowlists for the systems and sites they may contact. Central authorisation through your identity provider makes this manageable across many agents and tools; the MCP Enterprise-Managed Authorization extension is one example of that pattern6.

Log and detect. Record every agent action with the agent's identity and the user it acts for, and alert when an agent reaches a system or site it has never used. Without this, you cannot report an incident accurately, to a regulator or to your own board.

Prepare to report. Define what counts as a serious AI incident in your organisation, who decides, and how you would tell customers, providers and, where required, regulators. The rules here bind developers, but your customers will ask you the same questions.

Update contracts. Ask AI providers how they will notify you of incidents affecting your use, what evaluation access they may request, and what changes they plan for Australia.

Regulation will catch up with model developers. The agent you deployed last month is your responsibility today. Scope it, log it and be ready to explain what it did.

CTO take

Australia joins a growing list of jurisdictions putting frontier AI developers under specific obligations; see India's frontier AI law and New York's registration rules. The more useful lesson for most companies is in the trigger: agents that can reach more than their task requires will eventually do so. Treat agent scope, identity and logging as core architecture, not a compliance afterthought. See agentic infrastructure, the AI provider resilience case study and the Australia page.

FAQ

Common questions

What did Australia announce on 10 November 2026?

The government said it will legislate mandatory obligations for frontier AI developers, covering areas such as security controls, managing serious risks, independent evaluation, transparency and rapid reporting of serious incidents, overseen by a new regulator working with the Australian AI Safety Institute. The rules are not yet law.

Do the rules apply to companies that only use AI?

As announced, they target frontier AI developers. Companies building on those models are likely to feel them through contracts, incident notices and evaluation requirements, and the incident behind the rules is a deployment problem any company using agents should guard against.

How do you stop an AI agent going beyond its task?

Give each agent task-scoped permissions and short-lived credentials, restrict which systems and sites it can reach, require approval for sensitive actions, and log every action so you can detect and report when it strays.
