Microsoft 365 went down: what it broke, why it spread so far, and what to do before the next outage

On 12 November 2026, Microsoft 365 failed across regions for much of a working day: email, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot, and for some organisations the security tools that sit on top of them. Your core systems may have been healthy, yet work stopped because the tools people use to communicate, find information and sign in were down. This is what happened, why the impact was so wide, and a playbook for being ready next time, whatever suite you use.

What happened

Problems began with Exchange Online and spread to other Microsoft 365 services and regions on 12 November. Microsoft's advisories listed Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint Online, Microsoft 365 Copilot and Defender XDR among affected services, and users also reported OneDrive problems13. Microsoft said servers were failing automated health checks and dropping out of service, pushing load onto the rest, and that it had found no indication of malicious activity2. It paused deployments and worked on a configuration change to relieve an overwhelmed connection process3.

The disruption was felt worldwide, with users in Australia reporting problems into the next day4. Recovery was gradual and uneven, and Microsoft has promised a post-incident report with the root cause1. Business customers may be able to claim service credits, but generally need to ask5.

Why one outage reached so far

Most organisations no longer use a productivity suite only for email. Over a few years it has become the place where people talk, store documents, sign in, get AI help and often run security tooling. When it fails, the effects cascade.

The pattern is worth naming: the tools you use to manage an incident are often hosted by the supplier that is having the incident. Many teams discovered that their incident bridge, their contact list and their runbook all lived in Teams and SharePoint.

During an outage: the first hour

Avoid well-meant improvisation: staff forwarding company email to personal accounts, sharing files through unapproved tools or resetting passwords repeatedly. Outages are also a favourite moment for phishing that imitates the supplier's status messages, so warn staff to expect it.

Before the next one: the playbook

1. Map your dependencies. For each critical business process, list what it needs from the suite: email, chat, documents, identity, AI. Most organisations find identity is the biggest single point of failure, because it gates access to everything else.

2. Build an out-of-band channel and use it. Pick a messaging and paging tool from a different provider, with accounts for incident leaders, and use it for routine on-call so it works when needed.

3. Keep critical information outside the suite. Contact lists, escalation paths and top runbooks should exist somewhere that does not depend on it: a separate tool, an offline copy and a short printed card for on-call staff.

4. Plan emergency access. Keep a small number of monitored break-glass accounts that can reach critical systems if the main identity provider is unavailable, protected with hardware keys and tested regularly.

5. Detect independently. Do not rely on the supplier's status page or staff complaints. Simple synthetic checks against sign-in, mail and chat endpoints, run from outside your network, give you earlier warning and your own evidence6.

6. Give customers another route. A status page hosted elsewhere, SMS templates and a phone line mean customers hear from you even when email is down.

7. Decide what AI is allowed to be critical for. If teams rely on an AI assistant for routine work, decide what happens when it is unavailable, and make sure nothing that must happen depends on it alone.

8. Rehearse. Run a short exercise: "the suite is down for a day". It will expose gaps faster than any document review.

You cannot stop a supplier's outage. You can make sure it is an inconvenience rather than a crisis, by deciding in advance how you will talk, decide and serve customers without it.

Should you diversify away from one suite?

OptionBenefitCost
Stay on one suite, add fallbacksSimple, cheap, covers most riskFallbacks must be maintained and used
Separate identity providerSign-in survives a suite outageMore integration and licences
Second suite for critical teamsFull capability during outagesExpensive and confusing day to day

For most organisations, the first option, with careful attention to identity and emergency access, gives the best return. Regulated firms with strict recovery targets may need more; see the Singapore insurer case study and the AI provider resilience case study.

This week

  • Hold a 30-minute review: what stopped, what you improvised, what you would do differently.
  • Record the incident IDs and your impact, and decide whether to claim service credits.
  • Set up an out-of-band channel for incident leaders if you do not have one.
  • Export critical contact lists and runbooks to a location outside the suite.
  • When Microsoft's post-incident report is published, read it and update your plan.

CTO take

This outage was not a security incident, and it will be fixed. Its lasting value is as a free rehearsal: it showed every organisation exactly where it depends on one supplier, at no cost beyond a lost day. Use that evidence now, while people remember it, to fund the small fallbacks that turn the next outage into an inconvenience. FAQ

Common questions

What happened in the Microsoft 365 outage on 12 November 2026?

Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint Online, Microsoft 365 Copilot and other services were disrupted across regions. Microsoft pointed to servers failing health checks and an overwhelmed connection process, said it found no sign of malicious activity, and promised a post-incident report with the root cause.

Can we get compensation for a Microsoft 365 outage?

Business customers may be able to claim service credits under Microsoft's service level agreement, but they generally have to ask for them. Check your agreement and the incident IDs in the Microsoft 365 admin centre.

What is the most important thing to prepare for a SaaS outage?

A way to coordinate and communicate that does not depend on the failed suite: an out-of-band messaging channel, offline contact lists and runbooks, and emergency access that does not rely on the same identity provider.
