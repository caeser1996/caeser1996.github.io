Tech news, 6 November 2026: OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber, an AI stock selloff, Google in Wyoming, Micron pauses in Taiwan and an exploited Apple flaw

OpenAI released a cyber model that it rates at its highest risk tier and gates behind an application process. The same day, AI and chip stocks fell sharply as investors weighed the political backlash against data centres, Google answered that backlash with a $17 billion project that pays its own power bill, and a labour dispute paused a memory plant in Taiwan.

Today's brief covers stories from 4 to 5 November.

1. OpenAI releases GPT-6 Cyber to approved security teams

OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber on 5 November, a version of its GPT-6 Astra model built for authorised vulnerability research, and rated it Critical, the top cybersecurity level in its Preparedness Framework1. Access is limited to approved security teams through an application process, and OpenAI says a Firefox research campaign produced evidence for 81 new vulnerabilities that Mozilla classified as high severity12. Most performance figures so far come from OpenAI's own testing.

CTO take: models that find and confirm bugs at this level shorten the time between a flaw existing and someone exploiting it, whoever gets access first. The defensive response is the same as for earlier cyber models: faster patching, a current inventory of what you run, and capacity to handle more vulnerability reports at once. Security teams that qualify should apply and point the model at their own code. See defender-first AI models and vulnerability management.

2. AI and chip stocks lead a sharp market fall

The Nasdaq Composite fell about 5.5% on 5 November and the S&P 500 about 4.4%, with Xinhua linking the chip selloff to concerns that the midterm results could bring tighter restrictions on data centre construction3. Axios reported that the fall put the Nasdaq into correction territory4.

CTO take: one bad trading day does not change your roadmap, but it is a reminder that much AI pricing and capacity rests on investor confidence. If your plans assume AI costs keep falling and capacity keeps growing, test them against a slower path. Avoid long commitments to a single small provider, and keep the ability to move workloads; see model routing and planning around the AI capacity crunch.

3. Google plans a $17 billion data centre in Wyoming

Google announced a $17 billion data centre in Laramie County, its first in Wyoming, and said it will cover the full cost of the electricity the site uses5. WyoFile reports the project is to be partly powered by a planned fuel cell development with carbon capture, and that the site will use air cooling to protect local water supplies6.

CTO take: after this week's ballot results, paying for your own power and avoiding water use are becoming the price of local consent. Expect new capacity to arrive slowly and in places with spare energy. If you choose cloud regions for AI work, ask your providers where their new capacity is and when it lands; see the multi-region AI capacity case study and the US page.

4. Micron pauses a Taiwan memory plant after a union dispute

Bloomberg reported on 5 November that Micron has largely paused work on a roughly $4.5 billion plant in Taiwan after a dispute with its union over pay7. TheStreet reports the union is seeking a larger share of profits, the plant was meant to make memory for AI servers, and Micron says existing Taiwan operations are unaffected8.

CTO take: high-bandwidth memory is already one of the tightest parts of the AI supply chain, and this adds risk to future supply rather than today's. For most teams it means AI inference prices may stay high for longer, which makes efficiency work worth doing now: smaller models where they are good enough, caching and batching. See the AI memory squeeze and inference architecture and prompt caching.

5. An old Apple flaw is being exploited

CISA added CVE-2024-27834, a flaw affecting several Apple products that was patched in 2024, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 4 November, giving US federal agencies until 18 November to fix it9. The Hacker News reports it is being used to deliver information-stealing malware9.

CTO take: a two-year-old bug being exploited means attackers are counting on devices that were never updated. Check your device management reports for Apple devices on old operating system versions, including ones that can no longer be updated, and decide whether they should still reach company systems.

What I'd do this week

  • If your security team qualifies, apply for access to cyber-capable models and plan for more vulnerability reports.
  • Find Apple devices on outdated software and restrict or replace them.
  • Stress-test AI cost and capacity plans against slower growth and higher memory prices.
