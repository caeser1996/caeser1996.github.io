Technical due diligence for AI companies: the checklist I use for investors and acquirers

Classic technical due diligence asks whether the software is well built and the team can scale it. For AI companies those questions still matter, but a new set sits on top: is the AI actually good, is it defensible, is it safe, and does the company have the right to the data behind it?

A typical AI pitch deck shows a polished demo, impressive early revenue and a slide titled "proprietary AI". Diligence is where that slide meets reality. The traditional checklist (code quality, architecture, security, team, scalability) still applies. But AI companies add risks that a conventional review can miss entirely, and some of them go straight to valuation.

This is the checklist I work through when assessing an AI company for an investor or acquirer. It's organised as questions, with the evidence I'd expect to see.

1. Does the AI actually work, and how do they know?

  • Is there a maintained evaluation set? Real examples, labelled with expected outcomes, run on every model or prompt change. If quality is judged by demos and anecdotes, that's the single biggest red flag. I describe what good looks like in evaluation sets that predict production.
  • Is reliability measured, not just average accuracy? For agents, a single success rate hides inconsistency. Research on τ-bench introduced pass^k, the chance of succeeding on every one of k attempts, and found that agents were notably less consistent than their single-run success rates suggested5. See evaluating AI agents.
  • Is there production monitoring? Feedback, escalations, sampled reviews and drift checks, tied to the evaluation set.
  • Can they show improvement over time, with numbers from the same evaluation set across releases?

2. What is actually defensible?

  • Which parts are theirs? Separate the third-party model, open-source components and the company's own work: data pipelines, evaluation, domain logic, workflow integration, proprietary data.
  • What happens when the underlying model improves? If a better general model would make the product unnecessary, the moat is thin. If better models make the product better, that's healthier.
  • Is there a data advantage? Proprietary data, feedback loops from usage, or curated domain knowledge such as a governed ontology or knowledge graph are harder to copy than prompts.
  • How portable are they across model providers? A gateway and evaluation set that let them switch models are both a risk control and a negotiating position. See build vs buy for AI.

"We fine-tuned a model" is not a moat by itself. A moat is something a well-funded competitor couldn't reproduce in a quarter with the same public models.

3. Do they have the right to their data?

  • Provenance. Where did training, fine-tuning and retrieval data come from, under what licence or agreement?
  • Customer data use. Do contracts allow customer data to be used for training or evaluation? Is that used in practice, and is it consistent with the contract?
  • Personal and sensitive data. How is personal, health or financial data handled in prompts, logs, embeddings and evaluation sets? Residency matters for some markets, as in UAE health data and Saudi PDPL.
  • Deletion. Can they actually delete a customer's data from every store, including vector indexes and caches?

4. Is the AI secure and contained?

  • Known risk categories. Has the team assessed the product against a recognised list such as the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications1?
  • Prompt injection. The UK NCSC has argued that prompt injection may never be fully mitigated the way SQL injection was6, so the question is containment: what can a compromised agent actually do? See prompt injection defence.
  • Tool and data permissions. Are agent tools scoped per user, enforced outside the model, and logged? See securing MCP servers.
  • Tenant isolation. Can retrieval ever return another customer's data? Ask for the test that proves it can't.

5. Is the engineering organisation healthy?

  • Delivery metrics. Deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate and recovery time, with trends. DORA's 2025 research found that AI tends to amplify an organisation's existing strengths and weaknesses4, so a team leaning heavily on AI coding tools without good foundations may be accumulating risk; see DORA metrics in the AI era.
  • Key-person risk. Is the AI capability concentrated in one or two people? What's documented?
  • Decision records. Are major architecture choices written down, as in architecture decision records?

6. Do the unit economics hold?

  • Cost per outcome. Model, infrastructure and human review cost per resolved ticket, document or transaction, not just per token.
  • Sensitivity. What happens to margins if usage grows ten times, or a provider changes pricing?
  • Levers in use. Caching, routing and right-sized models, as in prompt caching and model routing. Unused levers are upside; no visibility at all is a risk.

7. What is the regulatory exposure?

  • Classification. Could any use case fall into a regulated or high-risk category under the EU AI Act3, or sector rules in health, finance or employment?
  • Governance evidence. An AI system register, risk assessments, incident handling and training. Frameworks such as the NIST AI RMF2 are a reasonable yardstick; see ISO 42001 or NIST AI RMF.
  • Customer commitments. What have they promised enterprise customers about AI, and can they evidence it?

Reading the results

No early-stage company will score well on everything, and that's not the point. Diligence should separate fixable gaps, such as missing monitoring or undocumented decisions, from structural problems, such as no data rights, no evaluation discipline or a product that a better general model would replace. The first belong in a post-investment plan with owners and dates. The second belong in the valuation conversation.

FAQ

Common questions

What is different about technical due diligence for AI companies?

In addition to code quality, architecture and team, diligence must assess model quality and how it's measured, dependence on third-party models, data rights and provenance, AI-specific security risks such as prompt injection, and regulatory exposure.

What is the biggest red flag in AI diligence?

Often the absence of a maintained evaluation set. Without one, a company can't show that its product works, that it improves over time, or that a model change won't break it.

How long does AI technical due diligence take?

It depends on scope, but a focused assessment typically combines document review, architecture and code walkthroughs, a review of evaluation evidence and interviews with technical leaders over a few weeks.
