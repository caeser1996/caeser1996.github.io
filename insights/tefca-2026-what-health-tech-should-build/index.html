TEFCA in 2026: what changed, and what health tech teams should build for

For years TEFCA was a framework people talked about more than used. In 2026 that changed: exchange volume grew sharply, a federal agency joined for benefits decisions, and enforcement showed the network has teeth.

TEFCA, the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement, connects US health information networks through Qualified Health Information Networks, or QHINs. It went live in December 2023. For most of its first two years, health tech teams could reasonably treat it as background. In 2026 it became something product and data architects need to plan around.

What changed this year

  • Volume. ASTP's February 2026 update reported more than 70,000 sites and more than 474 million documents shared since go-live1. Becker's, citing HHS, described nearly 500 million records exchanged by February 2026, up from around 10 million in January 20252. The counting methods differ, but the direction is unmistakable.
  • New uses. The Social Security Administration announced in February that it was connecting through a QHIN to speed up disability decisions3, and Becker's reported it went live that spring under a government benefits determination exchange purpose24.
  • Enforcement. Becker's also reported a March 2026 federal court order permanently barring a telehealth company, accused of requesting records under false treatment claims, from requesting records through TEFCA or Carequality2.

The enforcement action matters as much as the volume. A network that acts against misuse is one that health systems will trust with more data, and one where product teams must be able to prove their purpose for every request.

Exchange purposes are the design centre

Every TEFCA transaction carries a code identifying its exchange purpose: treatment, payment, health care operations, public health or individual access services, with room for more1. For a product team, that means purpose is not a policy document; it is a field on every request, and it has to be true.

Confirm the specifics against the current Common Agreement and standard operating procedures with counsel. The architectural point holds regardless: purpose has to be captured, enforced and auditable per request.

The FHIR roadmap

TEFCA started with document-based exchange. Its FHIR roadmap describes a staged move towards FHIR, from FHIR content over existing protocols through to FHIR-based exchange facilitated by QHINs5. Summaries differ in how they number the stages, so read the current roadmap from the Recognized Coordinating Entity before planning against it.

For architects, the practical advice is the same either way: build on FHIR internally now. Documents arriving today should land in a pipeline that can also accept FHIR resources tomorrow, using the patterns in FHIR Bulk Data pipelines and FHIR to OMOP.

What I would build now

  1. A purpose-aware request layer that attaches and checks the exchange purpose for every outbound query, with the evidence that justifies it.
  2. Audit trails that would satisfy a QHIN: who requested what, for which patient, under which purpose, and what came back.
  3. Document ingestion with structure, extracting coded data from clinical documents into a governed store rather than keeping them as opaque files.
  4. Identity and patient matching treated as first-class problems, since record location across networks depends on them.
  5. Controls on downstream AI use, so data received for one purpose isn't silently reused for another, in line with HIPAA security expectations.

Who should pay attention

Digital health vendors whose products depend on records from many providers, consumer health apps, payers and anyone building clinical AI that needs longitudinal data. Commentary on QHIN expansion suggests the network is becoming a practical route to data that used to need many point-to-point integrations6. The teams that design for purpose, audit and FHIR now will find it much easier to connect when their customers ask.

FAQ

Common questions

What is TEFCA?

The Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement, a US nationwide framework for health information exchange between networks called Qualified Health Information Networks (QHINs). It went live in December 2023.

What are TEFCA exchange purposes?

The permitted reasons for exchanging data, including treatment, payment, health care operations, public health and individual access services. Every transaction must carry a code identifying its exchange purpose. A government benefits determination purpose has also been used in 2026.

Does TEFCA use FHIR?

TEFCA began with document-based exchange, and its FHIR roadmap sets out a staged move towards FHIR-based exchange between and within networks. Summaries of the roadmap differ in detail, so check the current version from the Recognized Coordinating Entity.
