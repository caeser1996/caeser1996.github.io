Engineering metrics for boards: what to show, and what to leave out

Sooner or later a board asks: how is engineering doing? The answers that are easiest to produce, commits, story points, tickets closed, are the least useful, and some actively mislead. A board needs a handful of measures that connect engineering to outcomes, risk and money, explained the same way every quarter. Here is what I put in front of boards, and what I keep out.

The question behind the question

When a board asks how engineering is doing, it is rarely asking about engineering. It wants to know three things: are we spending this money well, are we exposed to something that could hurt us, and will the plan we approved actually ship? Engineering metrics are only useful in a board pack if they answer one of those.

The trouble is that the easiest numbers to produce answer none of them. Commit counts, story points, tickets closed and lines of code all measure activity. Repository activity graphs, such as commit activity over time4, are genuinely useful to a team understanding its own rhythm. In a board pack, they invite the wrong conversation: why was a week quiet, why did a spike happen, can we get more of the busy weeks.

The researchers behind the SPACE framework make the underlying point well: productivity has several dimensions, including satisfaction, performance, activity, communication and efficiency, and no single metric, least of all an activity count, captures it3. Activity numbers are also the easiest to game, and anything reported to a board will be optimised.

What I put in front of boards

Where the money goes. The single most useful engineering chart for a board is how engineering time splits between new product, keeping existing systems running, reducing risk and platform work. It turns "why is the roadmap slow?" into a decision about allocation, which is the board's job.

Speed and stability, as trends. DORA's four keys, deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and time to restore service, describe delivery without counting individual effort1. DORA's research ties them to organisational performance2, which is why they belong in front of a board. Show quarter-on-quarter trends and explain the movements; absolute numbers mean little across companies.

The top technical risks. Five at most, each with an owner, a plain-language impact and a date. Unsupported software, a single engineer who understands billing, an unrehearsed disaster recovery plan. Boards are good at risk; give them engineering risk in the form they already use.

Commitments. The status of the handful of initiatives the board approved, with honest confidence levels. Not the whole roadmap.

What I leave out, and why

MetricWhy it stays out of the board packWhere it belongs
Commits, lines of codeMeasures activity, easily inflatedNowhere as a target
Story points and velocityTeam-specific units, not comparable or stableTeam planning only
Tickets closedRewards splitting work, ignores valueTeam retrospectives
Individual productivity rankingsDamages collaboration and trustNowhere
AI tool usage countsAdoption is not impactEngineering leadership, alongside outcome measures

Every number in a board pack becomes a target. Before adding one, ask what behaviour you would get if the team optimised it perfectly.

AI coding tools have made this more urgent. Usage dashboards are easy to produce and look like progress. The question a board should ask is whether lead time, failure rate and allocation have moved, not how many suggestions were accepted. I cover the measurement side in DORA metrics for AI-assisted engineering.

How to introduce this without a reporting project

Consistency matters more than sophistication. A board that sees the same one-page view every quarter learns to read it and starts asking better questions. A different dashboard each time teaches it nothing.

The CTO's take

Good engineering reporting makes the board a better partner: it can see trade-offs, back risk reduction and hold leadership to outcomes rather than activity. Bad reporting does the opposite, inviting micromanagement of numbers that do not matter. If you are a founder or CTO preparing a board pack, start with allocation, risk and commitments, add DORA trends, and leave the activity charts with the teams. FAQ

Common questions

Should boards see DORA metrics?

Yes, in summary. Deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and recovery time describe delivery speed and stability without counting individual effort. Show trends and explain movements rather than raw numbers alone.

Why not report commits or story points?

They measure activity, vary with team habits and are easy to inflate. Once they appear in a board pack, teams learn to optimise them, and the board learns nothing about outcomes or risk.

What else belongs in an engineering board update?

Where engineering time and money go, the biggest technical risks with owners and dates, reliability against customer-facing targets, and progress on the few initiatives the board cares about.
