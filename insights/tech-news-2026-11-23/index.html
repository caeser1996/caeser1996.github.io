Tech news, 23 November 2026: Amazon and Decart, an open-weight Inkling 2 Mini, RTX Spark desktops and a ransomware claim against Nvidia

Amazon is reported to be close to buying Decart, whose software helps AI models run on AWS's own chips. Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini with open weights sized for a laptop, and Nvidia's RTX Spark compact desktops went on sale for local AI work. Nvidia also said it is investigating a possible incident at a third-party partner after a ransomware group listed it.

Today's brief covers stories from 20 to 22 November.

1. Amazon reportedly nears a deal for Decart

Amazon is in advanced talks to buy Decart, a startup whose software helps AI models run across different chips including AWS's own Trainium, in a deal reported at about $7.5 billion that could be announced soon, according to reporting citing the Financial Times1. Nothing has been announced, and reports note Decart was valued at $4 billion earlier in the year2.

CTO take: the strategic point is software that makes non-Nvidia chips easier to use. If it works, AWS customers get more realistic alternatives for inference, and more price competition. Do not plan around a rumour, but do keep your serving stack portable: standard model formats, a serving layer you control and evaluation sets that let you compare accelerators on your own workloads. See LLM inference serving and inference architecture under memory constraints.

2. Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini with open weights

Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Mini under Apache 2.0, a looped mixture-of-experts model whose middle layers run several times per token, with a reported memory footprint of about 20 GB3. Coverage says it accepts text and images and is pitched to run on well-equipped laptops; benchmark claims come from the lab and independent testers and should be checked against your own tasks4.

CTO take: capable open-weight models that run on a developer's machine change two things: private experimentation without sending data out, and cheaper fallbacks for routine tasks. Add it to your evaluation set alongside your current models, and record its lineage in your model inventory, as in model provenance. Licence terms matter: check the acceptable use policy as well as the Apache licence.

3. RTX Spark compact desktops go on sale

Nvidia said compact desktops built on its RTX Spark platform are now available from ASUS, Dell and MSI, with machines from Acer, GIGABYTE, HP and a Microsoft developer box to follow5. The Next Web reports prices starting at $4,399, with configurations up to 128 GB of unified memory6.

CTO take: machines like this make local model work practical for small teams: prototyping, fine-tuning small models and running evaluations without cloud queues or data leaving the building. They are not production infrastructure. Treat them as developer workstations, with disk encryption, device management and clear rules on which data may sit on them. See my notes on an AI architect's desktop bench.

4. Nvidia investigates a possible partner incident after a ransomware claim

Nvidia said it is investigating an incident that may have affected a third-party partner, after the Gentlemen ransomware group listed the company on its leak site, and that it has found no indication its own systems or networks were affected7. The group had not published evidence at the time of reporting.

CTO take: "a partner may be affected" is now the most common shape of a large company's incident. If you share data with partners, know exactly what each one holds, set expectations for notification in contracts and be ready to answer customers before the facts are complete. See vendor access as perimeter.

What I'd do this week

  • Check how portable your model serving is across accelerators and clouds.
  • Add a capable small open-weight model to your evaluation set as a fallback candidate.
  • Set rules for local AI workstations: encryption, management and permitted data.
  • Review what data each partner holds and how quickly they must tell you about incidents.
